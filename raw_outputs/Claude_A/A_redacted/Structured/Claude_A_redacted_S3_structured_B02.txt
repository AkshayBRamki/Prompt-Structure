CASE: M009

KEY FINDINGS:
A 13-year-old with a painless, well-demarcated, bony-hard swelling on the buccal right maxillary alveolar ridge (premolar–first molar). This points to a slow, benign bony process.
The overlying mucosa is intact, adjacent teeth are vital, and there is no trauma or parafunction history. This argues against odontogenic, inflammatory or reactive-traumatic causes.
CBCT shows an exophytic, mushroom-shaped radiodense mass continuous with both the cortical and trabecular bone. This is the hallmark of an exostosis or osteochondroma-type outgrowth rather than a separate tumour.
Histology shows bony trabeculae with intervening connective tissue, consistent with benign exostosis/hyperostosis.
There is no family history of Gardner syndrome, and the lesion is single. This makes a syndromic osteoma less likely.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Solitary buccal exostosis of the maxilla
Peripheral osteoma
Osteochondroma
Ossifying fibroma / fibrous dysplasia
Torus-like hyperostosis (atypical location)
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Buccal exostosis.
For: buccal alveolar site, bony-hard, well-demarcated, continuity with cortex and medulla, benign trabecular histology.
Against: unusual at age 13 (more typical in adults) and no parafunction history.
Peripheral osteoma.
For: a radiodense, pedunculated/mushroom shape.
Against: osteomas are typically compact or cancellous bone tumours, and alveolar ridge involvement is less typical. Histology was reported as exostosis/hyperostosis, and there is no Gardner context.
Osteochondroma.
For: a mushroom-shaped lesion with cortico-medullary continuity.
Against: no cartilaginous cap was described on histology, and it is rare in the maxillary alveolus.
Ossifying fibroma / fibrous dysplasia.
For: young age and maxillary expansion.
Against: no ground-glass or mixed density, an exophytic rather than expansile intraosseous shape, and the histology is not fibro-osseous.
Torus.
For: a benign hyperostosis.
Against: tori occur at the palatal midline or lingual mandible, not the buccal maxillary ridge.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Fibro-osseous lesions are excluded on imaging and histology.
Torus is excluded by location.
Osteochondroma is excluded by the absence of cartilage.
Osteoma is less likely given the histology and location.
I commit to a benign buccal exostosis.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Solitary buccal exostosis (benign hyperostosis) of the right maxillary alveolar ridge.

CASE: M010

KEY FINDINGS:
Prior D&C (uterine instrumentation) after a 15-week missed abortion. This is a classic risk factor for acquired vascular lesions.
Two months later: heavy bleeding with clots, presyncope, confusion and hemodynamic instability. This indicates a high-flow bleeding source.
TVUS shows an echogenic mass with increased flow, MRI shows serpiginous flow voids with enhancement, and CTA shows a vessel cluster with early venous filling (arteriovenous shunting).
Selective angiography identifies a feeding left uterine artery.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Acquired uterine arteriovenous malformation (AVM)
Retained products of conception (RPOC) with enhanced myometrial vascularity
Gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD)
Uterine artery pseudoaneurysm
Placental polyp
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Acquired AVM.
For: post-D&C timing, a hypervascular mass, serpiginous vessels, early venous filling on CTA, and a uterine artery feeder on angiography.
Against: overlapping ultrasound appearance with RPOC.
RPOC with enhanced vascularity.
For: an echogenic endometrial mass, the earlier ultrasound suggesting RPOC, and a recent pregnancy.
Against: RPOC does not cause arteriovenous shunting with early venous filling.
GTD.
For: post-pregnancy bleeding and a vascular mass.
Against: no β-hCG elevation or invasive mass was described, and the angiographic picture is of shunting rather than a tumour.
Pseudoaneurysm.
For: post-instrumentation timing and the bleeding pattern.
Against: imaging shows a vessel cluster rather than a single saccular outpouching.
Placental polyp.
For: an echogenic retained mass.
Against: no AV shunting.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Early venous filling with a discrete arterial feeder is diagnostic of arteriovenous communication. This excludes isolated RPOC and placental polyp.
GTD and pseudoaneurysm do not fit the morphology.
I commit to an acquired AVM. Coexisting retained tissue is possible but is not the primary bleeding source.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Acquired uterine arteriovenous malformation (post-D&C), supplied by the left uterine artery.

CASE: M011

KEY FINDINGS:
Subacute right lower quadrant (RLQ) pain, fever, weight loss and anemia, with a raised ESR and CRP. This suggests a chronic infectious or inflammatory ileocecal process rather than simple appendicitis.
Risk factors: IV drug use, alcohol, and an HIV diagnosis during admission. These indicate immunosuppression and TB risk.
Right upper lobe consolidation with tree-in-bud on CT is typical of active pulmonary TB with endobronchial spread.
Surgery found a perforated cecal mass with diffuse white small-bowel lesions and peritonitis.
Histology showed necrotizing (caseating) granulomatous colitis and lymphadenitis.
MTB PCR was positive.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Intestinal (ileocecal) tuberculosis with perforation and peritonitis, with pulmonary TB, in HIV
Crohn's disease
Acute appendicitis
Intestinal lymphoma (HIV-associated)
Mycobacterium avium complex or CMV enterocolitis
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Intestinal TB.
For: ileocecal location, necrotizing granulomas, positive MTB PCR, concurrent pulmonary TB, and HIV.
Against: none significant.
Crohn's disease.
For: RLQ mass, granulomas and weight loss.
Against: Crohn's granulomas are non-necrotizing, the PCR is positive, and there is lung involvement.
Appendicitis.
For: migrating pain and the ultrasound appearance.
Against: five days of fever with weight loss, and surgery found a cecal mass rather than an inflamed appendix.
Lymphoma.
For: HIV, a mass, B symptoms and cytopenias.
Against: histology shows granulomas, not lymphoma.
MAC/CMV.
For: advanced HIV.
Against: the PCR is specific for MTB, and the necrotizing granulomas fit MTB.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Histology combined with PCR excludes Crohn's disease, lymphoma and simple appendicitis.
I commit to disseminated (pulmonary and intestinal) TB in newly diagnosed HIV.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Ileocecal (intestinal) tuberculosis complicated by cecal perforation and peritonitis, with concurrent pulmonary TB, in newly diagnosed HIV infection.

CASE: M012

KEY FINDINGS:
An 84-year-old inpatient with sudden diffuse abdominal pain, hypotension and tachycardia. These indicate acute intra-abdominal hemorrhage.
Hemoglobin fell from 13.1 to 8.8 g/dL in 24 hours.
CTA showed active bleeding with hemoperitoneum, and splenectomy was performed, so the bleeding was splenic.
No trauma was reported.
Histology showed a 1 cm subcapsular nodule with no malignancy or infection. This is a benign intrinsic splenic lesion acting as the rupture point.
Comorbidities: renal failure (uremic platelet dysfunction), likely anticoagulant or antithrombotic exposure during admission, and pneumonia with coughing.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Atraumatic (spontaneous) splenic rupture secondary to a benign subcapsular lesion (e.g., splenic hemangioma or hamartoma)
Atraumatic splenic rupture due to infection
Atraumatic splenic rupture due to a malignant infiltrate
Splenic artery aneurysm rupture
Rupture of another solid organ or retroperitoneal hemorrhage
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Benign-lesion rupture.
For: hemoperitoneum from the spleen, a subcapsular nodule, and no malignancy or organisms.
Against: the lesion is small. Coagulopathy or uremia probably contributed.
Infectious rupture.
For: an inpatient with pneumonia.
Against: no organisms on histology.
Malignant rupture.
For: age.
Against: no malignancy on histology.
Splenic artery aneurysm.
For: hemoperitoneum.
Against: CTA and pathology localized the bleeding to the parenchyma and nodule.
Other organ.
Against: CTA and splenectomy confirmed a splenic source.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Pathology excludes infectious and neoplastic causes.
The imaging and surgical source excludes aneurysm and other organs.
I commit to atraumatic splenic rupture arising from a small benign subcapsular lesion. The exact histotype is redacted; a hemangioma or hamartoma is most likely.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Spontaneous (atraumatic) splenic rupture with hemoperitoneum, secondary to a benign subcapsular splenic lesion (most likely a hemangioma or hamartoma).

CASE: M013

KEY FINDINGS:
Class I molar relationship with a Class I skeletal base. The problem is dentoalveolar, not skeletal.
Upper anterior spacing with proclined, forwardly placed upper incisors, increased overjet and overbite, and a history of thumb-sucking. These point to proclined, spaced maxillary incisors.
Lower anterior crowding.
Convex profile, acute nasolabial angle, deep mentolabial sulcus, lip incompetence and a lower lip trap. These indicate dentoalveolar protrusion with soft-tissue consequences.
A high labial frenum, which contributes to spacing.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Angle Class I malocclusion, Dewey type 2 (proclined, spaced maxillary incisors) with lower crowding, on a Class I skeletal base
Bimaxillary dentoalveolar protrusion (Class I)
Class II division 1 malocclusion
Simple midline diastema from the frenum alone
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Class I, type 2.
For: Class I molars and skeletal base, upper spacing and proclination, increased overjet, and the thumb-sucking history.
Against: none major.
Bimaxillary protrusion.
For: convex profile, acute nasolabial angle and lip incompetence.
Against: lower crowding and a deep mentolabial sulcus suggest the protrusion is mainly maxillary.
Class II division 1.
For: increased overjet and lip trap.
Against: Class I molars and a Class I skeletal base.
Frenal diastema.
For: a high frenum.
Against: the spacing is generalized and accompanied by proclination.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Class II is excluded by the molar and skeletal relationships.
Isolated frenal diastema is excluded because the spacing is generalized.
I commit to Class I type 2 with maxillary dentoalveolar proclination.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Angle Class I malocclusion (Dewey type 2) on a Class I skeletal base, with proclined, spaced maxillary incisors (maxillary dentoalveolar protrusion), lower anterior crowding, increased overjet and overbite, and lip incompetence.

CASE: M014

KEY FINDINGS:
Dysuria and frequency since puberty, with a normal urinalysis and ultrasound. This suggests a neurogenic bladder rather than infection or a structural urological cause.
Progressive low back pain over the past year.
MRI shows a low-lying conus at S1–S2 tethered to a sacral lipoma, with hypoplastic sacrum and vertebral scalloping. This is occult spinal dysraphism with a tethered cord.
L4–L5 isthmic spondylolisthesis is a coexisting source of back pain.
The neurological exam is normal.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Tethered cord syndrome due to a sacral (filar or intradural) lipoma
Isthmic spondylolisthesis alone as the cause of the back pain
Interstitial cystitis or bladder pain syndrome
Recurrent or chronic UTI
Caudal regression or sacral agenesis spectrum
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Tethered cord syndrome.
For: a low conus with lipoma, bladder symptoms beginning in the growth spurt, progressive pain and associated dysraphic changes.
Against: a normal neurological exam, though this is common in adult presentations.
Spondylolisthesis alone.
For: it explains the back pain.
Against: it does not explain the long-standing bladder symptoms.
Interstitial cystitis.
For: dysuria and frequency with a negative urinalysis.
Against: an explanatory neural lesion is present.
UTI.
Against: a normal urinalysis and WBC count.
Caudal regression.
For: a hypoplastic sacrum.
Against: the conus is low rather than blunted and high, and there are no major agenesis or systemic features.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
UTI is excluded by laboratory findings.
Interstitial cystitis and spondylolisthesis alone do not explain the whole picture.
I commit to tethered cord syndrome, with spondylolisthesis as a contributing comorbidity.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Adult-presenting tethered cord syndrome secondary to a sacral spinal lipoma (occult spinal dysraphism), with associated L4–L5 isthmic spondylolisthesis.

CASE: M015

KEY FINDINGS:
Slowly progressive, adult-onset ataxia over two years, with scanning speech, nystagmus, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, rebound, broad-based gait, hypotonia and pendular reflexes. This is a pure pancerebellar syndrome.
Sensation and strength are intact and cognition is normal. There is no sensory neuropathy or pyramidal involvement.
Two affected siblings with unaffected parents and an unaffected next generation suggest autosomal recessive inheritance.
No toxic, alcoholic or drug exposure.
CT shows marked cerebellar atrophy.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Hereditary autosomal recessive cerebellar ataxia (pure cerebellar type, e.g., ARCA1/SYNE1)
Friedreich ataxia (late-onset)
Autosomal dominant spinocerebellar ataxia (SCA) with reduced penetrance
Ataxia with oculomotor apraxia or ataxia with vitamin E deficiency
Acquired ataxia (alcohol, paraneoplastic, MSA-C)
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
AR pure cerebellar ataxia.
For: sibling recurrence, unaffected parents, pure cerebellar signs, intact sensation and marked atrophy.
Against: genetic confirmation is unavailable.
Friedreich ataxia.
For: recessive inheritance.
Against: proprioception and reflexes are preserved, and there is prominent cerebellar atrophy (cerebellar atrophy is minimal in Friedreich ataxia).
AD SCA.
For: adult onset and cerebellar atrophy.
Against: unaffected parents.
AOA/AVED.
For: recessive inheritance.
Against: onset is late, and there is no neuropathy, oculomotor apraxia or proprioceptive loss.
Acquired causes.
Against: no exposures, a familial pattern and no autonomic features.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Acquired causes are excluded by the history and family history.
Friedreich ataxia and AVED are excluded by intact sensation and reflexes.
Dominant SCA is less likely given the pedigree.
I commit to hereditary AR cerebellar ataxia. Genetic testing is needed to name the gene.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Hereditary (likely autosomal recessive) pure cerebellar ataxia with marked cerebellar atrophy.

CASE: M016

KEY FINDINGS:
A 66-year-old with subacute, rapidly progressive encephalopathy, aphasia, hallucinations and weakness over months, ending in death. This indicates an aggressive diffuse CNS process.
MRI shows diffuse non-enhancing T2/FLAIR white-matter hyperintensities without mass effect, a picture of infiltrative disease that can mimic leukoencephalopathy.
Elevated CSF IL-10 with pleocytosis is a strong marker of B-cell CNS lymphoma.
Autoimmune, infectious and paraneoplastic workups were negative.
Later imaging showed new diffusion restriction and hyperperfusion in the caudate and hippocampus, with high choline and low NAA on spectroscopy. These are hypercellular tumour foci.
Autopsy found CD20-positive B cells diffusely, with superimposed focal lesions.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Lymphomatosis cerebri (diffuse infiltrative primary CNS DLBCL) with superimposed focal primary CNS lymphoma
Intravascular large B-cell lymphoma
Gliomatosis cerebri / diffuse glioma
Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease
Autoimmune or paraneoplastic encephalitis, or PML
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Lymphomatosis cerebri.
For: diffuse non-enhancing lesions, raised IL-10, CD20-positive infiltrate, and later focal hypercellular lesions.
Against: none decisive.
Intravascular lymphoma.
For: B-cell, subacute course.
Against: the infiltrate is parenchymal rather than confined to vessels, and there was no multifocal infarct pattern.
Gliomatosis.
For: diffuse non-enhancing T2 change and high choline.
Against: the cells were CD20-positive B cells.
CJD.
For: rapid decline and caudate/hippocampal diffusion restriction.
Against: hyperperfusion, high choline, pleocytosis, raised IL-10 and the autopsy findings.
Autoimmune encephalitis / PML.
For: subacute encephalopathy with hippocampal involvement.
Against: negative workups and the lymphoma histology.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Autopsy, CD20 positivity and IL-10 exclude glioma, CJD and autoimmune encephalitis.
The parenchymal pattern disfavors intravascular lymphoma.
I commit to lymphomatosis cerebri.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Lymphomatosis cerebri (diffuse infiltrative primary CNS diffuse large B-cell lymphoma) with superimposed focal primary CNS lymphoma in the caudate and hippocampus.
