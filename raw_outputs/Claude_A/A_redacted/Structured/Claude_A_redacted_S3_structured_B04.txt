CASE: M025

KEY FINDINGS:
CSF opening pressure of 27.2 cm H₂O with normal CSF contents proves raised intracranial pressure without infection or inflammation.
Normal MRI and MRV exclude a mass, hydrocephalus and venous sinus thrombosis, so the raised pressure is idiopathic.
Typical symptoms of raised pressure are present: morning headaches and pulsatile tinnitus.
BMI is 30.14 and the patient is a woman of reproductive age, the typical IIH demographic.
There is no disc swelling on fundus photography or OCT. Highly myopic, tilted discs with peripapillary atrophy and a crowded scleral canal can resist or mask papilledema.
Intraocular pressure is mildly raised (23/25 mmHg), but RNFL is normal and the discs are not cupped, which argues against glaucomatous damage.
Normal macula and normal ERG exclude a retinal cause of the field loss.
Normal autoimmune and antibody panels make an inflammatory optic neuropathy unlikely.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Idiopathic intracranial hypertension without papilledema (IIHWOP), masked by high myopia
Myopic glaucoma / ocular hypertension
Myopic degeneration-related field loss
Chronic migraine with medication-overuse headache
Secondary intracranial hypertension (venous thrombosis or obstruction, mass)
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
IIHWOP
For: high opening pressure with normal CSF, normal imaging, headache, pulsatile tinnitus, obesity, female, recent worsening.
Against: no visible papilledema, though this is explained by the myopic disc anatomy.
Myopic glaucoma
For: raised IOP, high myopia as a risk factor, constricted fields.
Against: normal RNFL thickness; it does not explain the raised ICP, headaches or tinnitus.
Myopic degeneration
For: long-standing high myopia, peripapillary atrophy.
Against: normal macula and ERG; it does not explain the raised ICP.
Migraine/MOH
For: history of chronic migraine.
Against: objectively raised CSF pressure, pulsatile tinnitus.
Secondary intracranial hypertension
For: raised ICP.
Against: normal MRI/MRV, normal CSF contents, normal labs.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Secondary causes are excluded by normal imaging and CSF.
Glaucoma and myopic degeneration cannot explain the raised ICP and are not supported by RNFL, macular or ERG findings.
Migraine alone does not produce a pressure of 27.2 cm H₂O.
Commitment: IIH, in which the high myopia prevents disc swelling.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Idiopathic intracranial hypertension without papilledema (papilledema masked by high myopia), with coexisting ocular hypertension.

CASE: M026

KEY FINDINGS:
The patient is a young adult man; the leading candidate tumor is strongly male-predominant.
Imaging is discordant across modalities:
Bland Bosniak I appearance on CT
Hyperechoic on ultrasound, from the many tiny cyst–wall interfaces
Bosniak IV on MRI, with nodular enhancement
This discordance is characteristic of a lesion made of tiny cysts.
Histology shows a multicystic architecture with fibrotic, hypocellular stroma.
The main mimics have been explicitly excluded: MEST (mixed epithelial and stromal tumor), AMLEC (angiomyolipoma with epithelial cysts), ACD-RCC (acquired cystic disease–associated RCC) and oncocytoma.
There is no history of end-stage renal disease or dialysis, which also argues against ACD-RCC.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Tubulocystic renal cell carcinoma
Multilocular cystic renal neoplasm of low malignant potential
Mixed epithelial and stromal tumor / adult cystic nephroma
Cystic papillary RCC
Oncocytoma with tubulocystic pattern
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Tubulocystic RCC
For: male predominance; multicystic architecture with thin, hypocellular fibrotic septa; hyperechoic on US yet "simple" on CT; Bosniak IV on MRI; its histologic differential is exactly the list that was excluded.
Against: definitive confirmation would need cytology showing eosinophilic hobnail cells and prominent nucleoli, which is not reported.
Multilocular cystic neoplasm of low malignant potential
For: multicystic architecture with fibrous septa.
Against: septa contain clear cells; this tumor typically appears as a complex cyst rather than a hyperechoic solid-looking lesion on US.
MEST/cystic nephroma
For: cysts with stroma.
Against: explicitly excluded; these tumors have cellular, ovarian-type stroma and usually occur in perimenopausal women.
Cystic papillary RCC
For: enhancing nodule.
Against: architecture is multicystic with no described papillae.
Oncocytoma
Against: explicitly excluded.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
MEST, AMLEC, ACD-RCC and oncocytoma are excluded on histology and clinical context.
Multilocular cystic neoplasm and papillary RCC fit the stroma and architecture poorly.
The combination of male sex, discordant imaging, and multicystic architecture with hypocellular fibrotic stroma points to tubulocystic RCC.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Tubulocystic renal cell carcinoma.

CASE: M027

KEY FINDINGS:
Young man with progressive voiding symptoms and a maximum flow of 4 mL/s, indicating severe obstruction.
A 6Fr catheter could not be passed.
There is no history of STI, trauma or instrumentation, so common acquired causes of stricture are absent.
An annular (ring-like) stenosis was found in the bulbar urethra. This pattern is typical of congenital stenosis (Cobb's collar / Moormann's ring).
A polypoid lesion lay behind the stenosis.
Resection cured the obstruction, with flow rising to 28 mL/s.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Fibroepithelial urethral polyp with congenital annular bulbar stenosis (Cobb's collar)
Inverted papilloma of the urethra
Nephrogenic adenoma
Condyloma acuminatum of the urethra
Urethral carcinoma
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Fibroepithelial polyp + Cobb's collar
For: young age, congenital-looking annular ring, benign-behaving polypoid lesion, cure after resection.
Against: these polyps are more often found in the posterior urethra in children.
Inverted papilloma
For: polypoid lesion.
Against: usually seen in older men and in the prostatic or bulbar urethra; does not explain the annular stenosis.
Nephrogenic adenoma
For: polypoid lesion.
Against: needs prior injury, instrumentation or infection, none of which occurred.
Condyloma
For: polypoid lesion.
Against: no STI history and normal external genitalia.
Urethral carcinoma
Against: age 25, no risk factors, durable cure after simple resection.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
The absence of trauma, infection and instrumentation excludes nephrogenic adenoma and condyloma, as well as an acquired stricture.
Malignancy is implausible.
Commitment: a benign congenital lesion, namely a fibroepithelial polyp associated with congenital bulbar annular stenosis.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Fibroepithelial polyp of the bulbar urethra associated with congenital annular bulbar urethral stenosis (Cobb's collar).

CASE: M028

KEY FINDINGS:
Large adnexal mass with peritoneal disease: ascites, omental induration and pelvic sidewall lesions.
CA-125 and CA 19-9 are both markedly elevated.
Abnormal uterine bleeding is present.
There are synchronous endometrioid carcinomas in the ovary and the endometrium; the endometrial tumor is stage IA with superficial invasion.
NGS shows shared mutations, including an MSH6 hotspot, MSI-H and high tumor mutation burden, and proves clonality. This means the two tumors are one cancer that has spread, not two independent primaries.
Germline MMR testing is negative, so this is a sporadic (somatic) MMR-deficient tumor and not Lynch syndrome.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Endometrial endometrioid adenocarcinoma (MSI-H) metastatic to the ovary and appendix
Synchronous independent primary endometrial and ovarian endometrioid carcinomas
Primary ovarian endometrioid carcinoma metastatic to the endometrium
Primary appendiceal adenocarcinoma metastatic to the ovary
Lynch syndrome–associated synchronous cancers
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Endometrial primary with ovarian metastasis
For: proven clonality; the molecular analysis identifies the uterus as the origin; abnormal bleeding.
Against: the uterine tumor is small and only superficially invasive, while the ovarian tumor is huge. However, low-volume endometrial primaries can still spread this way.
Independent synchronous primaries
For: classic clinicopathologic pattern (low-stage endometrial tumor with an ovarian tumor).
Against: clonality disproves independent origin.
Ovarian primary
For: tumor bulk and higher TMB in the ovary.
Against: the molecular evolutionary analysis assigns the uterus as the primary.
Appendiceal primary
For: appendiceal thickening, raised CA 19-9.
Against: endometrioid histology and clonality with the uterine tumor.
Lynch syndrome
For: MSI-H, MSH6 alteration.
Against: germline MMR testing is negative.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Clonality excludes independent primaries.
Directionality of the molecular data excludes an ovarian primary.
Histology excludes an appendiceal primary.
Germline testing excludes Lynch syndrome.
Commitment: a single endometrial primary with metastases. Because of the extra-uterine abdominal spread, this should be restaged as an advanced endometrial cancer rather than an early one.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Sporadic MMR-deficient/MSI-H (somatic MSH6) endometrioid endometrial adenocarcinoma with metastases to the ovary and appendix/peritoneum. This is advanced-stage endometrial cancer, not a synchronous ovarian primary.

CASE: M029

KEY FINDINGS:
EBV-HLH was treated and followed by a haploidentical stem cell transplant, with complete donor chimerism afterward.
At 11 months, EBV DNA was positive in plasma, PBMCs and CSF, with spinal cord swelling. This responded to rituximab, which indicates B-cell-driven EBV disease (CNS PTLD or EBV encephalomyelitis).
At 17 and 21 months, recurrent subcutaneous masses appeared with EBV-positive NK/T cells and FDG-avid disease on PET.
Complete remission followed L-GDP plus a PD-1 inhibitor, which is a standard regimen for NK/T-cell lymphoma.
Whole-exome sequencing found no germline HLH predisposition.
Because chimerism is fully donor, the NK/T-cell tumor is donor-derived.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Post-transplant EBV-positive extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (donor-derived), preceded by CNS EBV-PTLD
Systemic chronic active EBV disease (T/NK type) relapsing after transplant
Recurrent EBV-HLH
Monomorphic T/NK-cell PTLD, other subtype (e.g., PTCL-NOS)
Subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
ENKTL post-transplant
For: EBV-positive NK/T infiltrates forming tumor masses; PET avidity; recurrence; response to an ENKTL regimen.
Against: nasal origin is not described, though extranasal subcutaneous disease does occur.
CAEBV
For: EBV-HLH background, persistent EBV infection.
Against: discrete destructive tumor masses indicate a lymphoma.
Recurrent HLH
Against: no cytopenias or hemophagocytosis were reported; masses are present.
Other T/NK PTLD
For: post-transplant setting, EBV positivity.
Against: the NK/T phenotype with subcutaneous extranodal sites fits ENKTL better.
SPTCL
For: subcutaneous lesions.
Against: SPTCL is EBV-negative and of αβ T-cell origin.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
SPTCL is excluded by EBV positivity.
HLH is excluded by the clinical picture.
CAEBV does not account for true tumor masses.
Commitment: EBV-positive NK/T-cell lymphoma as a donor-derived post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder, following an earlier rituximab-responsive CNS EBV-PTLD.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Donor-derived EBV-positive extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (T/NK-cell PTLD) after haploidentical HSCT for EBV-HLH, preceded by CNS (spinal cord) EBV-PTLD.

CASE: M030

KEY FINDINGS:
Elderly, immunocompromised patient (metastatic lung cancer on alectinib, recent pleural drainage).
Sepsis picture: fever, confusion, neutrophilia of 23,900, raised procalcitonin and CRP.
Self-limited diarrhea two months earlier suggests prolonged intestinal carriage of an enteric pathogen.
The same organism was found in blood cultures and in stool (FilmArray panel), linking the bacteremia to a gut source.
The pleural culture was negative, excluding an empyema source.
GGT is raised (225 U/L), likely reflecting liver metastases or cholestasis.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Non-typhoidal Salmonella bacteremia (invasive salmonellosis)
Campylobacter bacteremia
Yersinia enterocolitica bacteremia
Plesiomonas/Vibrio bacteremia
Empyema or catheter-related bacteremia after pleural drainage
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Non-typhoidal Salmonella
For: the most common panel-detectable enteric organism to cause bacteremia, especially in older, cancer-bearing patients; prolonged carriage after diarrhea.
Against: no endovascular focus has yet been documented, so one should be sought.
Campylobacter
For: can cause bacteremia in immunocompromised hosts.
Against: C. jejuni bacteremia is rare and usually occurs during the acute diarrheal illness.
Yersinia
For: bacteremia occurs in older patients with comorbidities and iron overload.
Against: no iron overload; less common.
Plesiomonas/Vibrio
Against: no travel and no seafood exposure reported.
Pleural source
Against: negative pleural culture; concordant stool detection points to the gut.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
The pleural source is excluded by culture.
Vibrio/Plesiomonas are unlikely without relevant exposures.
Campylobacter and Yersinia are less likely given the timing and epidemiology.
Commitment: invasive non-typhoidal salmonellosis in an immunocompromised host, with possible endovascular or metastatic foci to be looked for.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Non-typhoidal Salmonella enterica bacteremia (invasive salmonellosis) from intestinal carriage, in a patient with metastatic lung cancer on alectinib.

CASE: M031

KEY FINDINGS:
Large, necrotic, infiltrative peritoneal or mesenteric mass adherent to the stomach, pancreas and transverse colon.
No metastases and no fever.
Intraoperatively the mass was non-malignant and well vascularized, yet locally aggressive enough to need multivisceral resection.
No recurrence at one year.
The behavior is locally aggressive but non-metastasizing, which suggests an intermediate-grade soft-tissue lesion.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Mesenteric desmoid-type fibromatosis
Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor
Gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST)
Localized (unicentric) Castleman disease
Sclerosing mesenteritis
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Desmoid fibromatosis
For: infiltration into adjacent fat and organs, large size, no metastases, benign on frozen section.
Against: necrosis is unusual, though large tumors can degenerate; recurrence is common, so one year is a short follow-up.
Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor
For: infiltrative mass in a middle-aged adult, benign appearance.
Against: no fever, anemia or inflammatory syndrome.
GIST
For: large necrotic mass next to the stomach.
Against: intraoperative pathology non-malignant; it is extraluminal and infiltrative rather than arising from the bowel wall.
Castleman disease
For: well-vascularized and benign.
Against: usually well circumscribed, not infiltrative, and rarely necrotic.
Sclerosing mesenteritis
For: infiltrates mesenteric fat.
Against: rarely forms a large necrotic mass needing gastrectomy.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
GIST is unlikely given the non-malignant intraoperative pathology and extramural infiltration.
Castleman disease and sclerosing mesenteritis do not match the morphology.
IMT lacks inflammatory features.
Commitment: desmoid-type fibromatosis, accepting that definitive histology could favor IMT.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Intra-abdominal (mesenteric) desmoid-type fibromatosis.

CASE: M032

KEY FINDINGS:
Both patients had venous sinus stenting (VSS) for IIH or transverse-sigmoid stenosis.
After stenting, both developed new symptoms on the treated side: headache, pulsatile tinnitus and hearing change.
Follow-up angiograms in both showed a lesion that was successfully obliterated with Onyx embolization. Onyx obliteration is the standard treatment for an arteriovenous shunt, so this points to a new dural arteriovenous fistula forming next to the stented sinus.
Case 1 has a prior history of DVT and PE (thrombophilic tendency), and venous hypertension or thrombosis is a recognized trigger for dural AVF.
DIFFERENTIAL:
De novo dural arteriovenous fistula of the transverse-sigmoid sinus after stenting
In-stent or stent-adjacent stenosis with recurrent IIH
Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis / in-stent thrombosis
Stent-related headache (periprocedural)
Pseudoaneurysm or vascular injury from the procedure
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Dural AVF
For: new pulsatile tinnitus and headache on the treated side; angiographic lesion cured by Onyx; known association with sinus instrumentation or thrombosis.
Against: none significant.
Restenosis/recurrent IIH
For: recurrent headache.
Against: Onyx embolization does not treat stenosis.
Venous thrombosis
For: thrombotic history in case 1.
Against: treated by embolization rather than anticoagulation; no thrombus described.
Stent headache
Against: an angiographic lesion was found that needed treatment.
Pseudoaneurysm
For: procedural vessel injury is possible.
Against: an access-related injury would not sit at the sinus, and pseudoaneurysms usually do not present with pulsatile tinnitus.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
The Onyx treatment and the new pulsatile, ipsilateral symptoms exclude stenosis, thrombosis and stent headache as primary explanations.
Commitment: a stent-associated dural AVF in both patients.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: De novo dural arteriovenous fistula at the transverse-sigmoid sinus after venous sinus stenting (both cases), treated with Onyx embolization.
