CASE: M073

KEY FINDINGS:
25 years of chronic alcoholism, recently heavier (2 L red wine/day), with severe malnutrition, low folate (B9), macrocytosis and raised liver enzymes. This is the classic setting for nutritional and toxic demyelination.
Subacute course (10 days) of confusion, stupor, dysarthria, poor comprehension, astasia-abasia and urinary incontinence. This points to frontal and callosal disconnection.
Grasp reflexes, gestural perseveration, inability to manipulate objects and spastic hyperreflexia. These are frontal release signs, apraxia and pyramidal involvement.
CT/MRI show symmetric T2/FLAIR and DWI hyperintensity of the entire corpus callosum with low ADC (cytotoxic edema, acute phase), plus bilateral frontal cortical hyperintensity. Normal MR angiography argues against a vascular cause.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Marchiafava-Bignami disease (MBD)
Wernicke encephalopathy
Osmotic demyelination syndrome (central/extrapontine myelinolysis)
Cytotoxic lesion of the corpus callosum from other causes (e.g., hypoglycemia, seizures, infection)
Callosal infarction or demyelinating disease (MS/ADEM)
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
MBD
For: chronic heavy red-wine drinking, malnutrition, folate deficiency, diffuse symmetric callosal lesions with restricted diffusion, interhemispheric disconnection and frontal signs. The frontal cortical lesions fit Morel's laminar sclerosis, which is associated with MBD.
Against: none significant.
Wernicke encephalopathy
For: alcoholism, malnutrition, confusion, ataxia.
Against: no ophthalmoplegia or nystagmus. MRI spares the medial thalami, mammillary bodies and periaqueductal region, and instead shows callosal lesions.
Osmotic demyelination
For: alcoholism and malnutrition are risk factors.
Against: no sodium disturbance or rapid correction is reported, the pons is not involved, and diffuse callosal involvement is atypical.
Other cytotoxic callosal lesions
For: restricted diffusion in the corpus callosum.
Against: these are usually small, reversible splenial lesions, not panc-callosal lesions with cortical involvement. No hypoglycemia, seizures or infection is reported.
Infarction or demyelination
Against: MRA is normal, the lesions are symmetric and diffuse, and the context is nutritional.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Wernicke encephalopathy is excluded by the absence of eye signs and of its typical MRI topography. Coexisting thiamine deficiency should still be treated empirically.
Osmotic demyelination is excluded because there is no sodium history and no pontine lesion.
Infarction, MS/ADEM and other cytotoxic callosal lesions are excluded by the imaging pattern, normal MRA and clinical context.
I commit to MBD with associated frontal cortical involvement.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Marchiafava-Bignami disease (acute form, whole corpus callosum, with frontal cortical/Morel-type involvement) in chronic alcoholism with malnutrition and folate deficiency.

CASE: M074

KEY FINDINGS:
A young woman (39) with a 4.7 cm renal tumor called "papillary RCC" on morphology alone, with no immunohistochemistry (IHC). Papillary-looking RCCs in young patients are frequently misclassified.
Recurrence as retroperitoneal masses 15 years after radical nephrectomy, with mild enhancement and cystic change. Very late indolent recurrence is characteristic of certain translocation-associated RCCs.
Diagnosis now required IHC, which implies a subtype that is not recognizable on morphology alone.
DIFFERENTIAL:
MiT family translocation RCC (Xp11.2/TFE3-rearranged), late metastatic recurrence
Late recurrence of true papillary RCC
Clear cell RCC late recurrence (misclassified primary)
Fumarate hydratase (FH)-deficient RCC
Primary retroperitoneal neoplasm, unrelated (e.g., sarcoma)
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Xp11.2/TFE3 translocation RCC
For: young female, papillary architecture mimicking papillary RCC, original diagnosis without IHC, and the well-described very late (decades) metastases, often to retroperitoneal nodes. Diagnosis requires TFE3 IHC or FISH.
Against: no direct molecular result is shown (redacted).
True papillary RCC recurrence
For: original label.
Against: 15-year latency is unusual, and confirmation would not have needed a "new" IHC-defined entity.
Clear cell RCC
For: late recurrence is known.
Against: the original morphology was papillary.
FH-deficient RCC
For: papillary morphology, young patient.
Against: typically aggressive with early metastasis; no family history of leiomyomas.
Unrelated retroperitoneal neoplasm
For: retroperitoneal soft tissue location.
Against: prior RCC in the nephrectomy bed and IHC confirmation linking the masses to a defined entity.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
FH-deficient RCC is excluded by the indolent course.
An unrelated tumor is less likely given the location and IHC-based diagnosis.
Typical papillary or clear cell RCC fit the late indolent recurrence and the need for IHC reclassification less well.
I commit to translocation RCC.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Xp11.2 (TFE3) translocation renal cell carcinoma, originally misdiagnosed as papillary RCC, with late retroperitoneal recurrence/metastasis.

CASE: M075

KEY FINDINGS:
Paroxysmal dry cough for one week, severe enough to prevent a 6-minute walk test.
Completely normal exam, oxygen saturation, chest X-ray, HRCT, spirometry, echocardiogram and eosinophil count. This excludes parenchymal, cardiac and fixed obstructive disease.
Atopic background (lifelong rhinitis) and occupational wood-dust exposure (woodcarving factory). These are risk factors for airway hyperresponsiveness and occupational asthma.
Myasthenia gravis on steroids and recent rituximab. This means immunosuppression, raising concern for infection or drug toxicity.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Cough-variant asthma (atopic or occupational, wood-dust related)
Upper airway cough syndrome (allergic rhinitis/postnasal drip)
Rituximab-induced lung toxicity (early ILD/pneumonitis)
Opportunistic or viral respiratory infection (e.g., Pneumocystis, viral bronchitis)
Non-asthmatic eosinophilic bronchitis or gastroesophageal reflux cough
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Cough-variant asthma
For: atopy, wood-dust exposure, paroxysmal dry cough, normal imaging. Spirometry is often normal, and diagnosis needs bronchial provocation testing.
Against: normal eosinophils, and steroid therapy might be expected to suppress it.
Upper airway cough syndrome
For: allergic rhinitis history.
Against: no current nasal symptoms or exam findings are described.
Rituximab lung toxicity
For: rituximab one month earlier.
Against: normal HRCT, normal saturation, no dyspnea or fever.
Opportunistic or viral infection
For: immunosuppression.
Against: afebrile, normal oxygen saturation, normal HRCT (Pneumocystis would usually show ground glass), normal blood count.
Eosinophilic bronchitis or GERD
For: chronic-cough mimics.
Against: normal eosinophils, acute onset, no reflux symptoms or obesity-related symptoms reported.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Rituximab toxicity and infection are made unlikely by normal HRCT, oxygen saturation and labs.
Cardiac and parenchymal causes are excluded by echocardiography and imaging.
Upper airway cough syndrome and GERD lack supporting features.
I commit to cough-variant asthma. This should be confirmed with a methacholine challenge and FeNO, and by response to inhaled corticosteroid/bronchodilator.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Cough-variant asthma (likely atopic, possibly occupational wood-dust related), pending confirmation by bronchial provocation testing.

CASE: M076

KEY FINDINGS:
One month of parenteral nutrition with intestinal obstruction. Thiamine is depleted unless supplemented.
Classic triad: ophthalmoplegia (multidirectional gaze limitation, diplopia), ataxia (unsteady gait, positive Romberg) and mental change (slowed comprehension and responses).
Atypical features: pinpoint pupils with preserved light reflex, refractory hiccups (periventricular/area postrema involvement) and reduced reflexes (thiamine-related neuropathy).
Thiamine level is low.
MRI evolves from normal to bilateral thalamic and periaqueductal hyperintensities, then improves with time/treatment.
Lumbar puncture shows no albuminocytologic dissociation, and EMG is normal. AQP-4 antibody is negative.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Wernicke encephalopathy
Miller Fisher syndrome / Guillain-Barré variant
AQP-4 neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (area postrema/diencephalic syndrome)
Bilateral thalamic infarction (artery of Percheron) or deep venous thrombosis
Myasthenia gravis or other neuromuscular ophthalmoparesis
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Wernicke encephalopathy
For: all of the findings above, including resolution of lesions.
Against: pinpoint pupils are atypical but are described.
Miller Fisher syndrome
For: ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, areflexia.
Against: no albuminocytologic dissociation, normal EMG, MRI lesions, low thiamine, altered mentation.
NMOSD
For: hiccups and periaqueductal/diencephalic lesions.
Against: AQP-4 negative, clear nutritional cause.
Percheron infarct or venous thrombosis
For: bilateral thalamic lesions.
Against: subacute course, periaqueductal involvement, rapid improvement, low thiamine.
Myasthenia gravis
For: ophthalmoparesis.
Against: pupils involved, ataxia, encephalopathy, MRI lesions.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Miller Fisher syndrome is excluded by the CSF and EMG findings.
NMOSD is excluded by the negative antibody and context.
Vascular causes are excluded by the time course, topography and reversibility.
Myasthenia gravis is excluded by the pupil, central and MRI findings.
I commit to Wernicke encephalopathy.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Non-alcoholic Wernicke encephalopathy due to thiamine deficiency during prolonged parenteral nutrition, with atypical features (miosis, intractable hiccups).

CASE: M077

KEY FINDINGS:
Four months of recurrent vomiting, dysphagia and pancreatitis with 60-lb weight loss. This is severe malnutrition.
Acute diplopia, esotropia (abducens palsy), horizontal nystagmus, ataxic wide-based gait and altered course. These are Wernicke signs.
Bilateral vision loss to light perception only, hearing loss/tinnitus, areflexia and leg numbness. These are atypical Wernicke features (optic neuropathy, auditory involvement) plus dry beriberi neuropathy.
Profound thiamine deficiency (<7), with multiple other vitamin deficiencies (A, K, D, folate).
MRI shows bilateral medial thalamic and periaqueductal T2 hyperintensity, the typical Wernicke pattern.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Wernicke encephalopathy (with optic neuropathy, hearing loss and thiamine-deficiency polyneuropathy)
Miller Fisher syndrome / Guillain-Barré syndrome
Neuromyelitis optica spectrum or MOG-associated disease
Susac syndrome
Mitochondrial disease or vitamin A–related optic neuropathy
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Wernicke encephalopathy
For: severe malnutrition, very low thiamine, classic signs and classic MRI pattern. Visual and hearing loss are reported in severe non-alcoholic cases.
Against: none substantial.
Miller Fisher / GBS
For: ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, areflexia.
Against: MRI lesions, vision and hearing loss, deficiency state.
NMOSD / MOG disease
For: vision loss and area postrema–like vomiting.
Against: thalamic/periaqueductal pattern in a deficiency context, hearing loss.
Susac syndrome
For: visual and hearing loss in a young woman.
Against: lesions are thalamic and periaqueductal, not callosal; deficiency explains the picture.
Mitochondrial disease or vitamin A deficiency
For: multisystem features; vitamin A deficiency causes visual problems.
Against: these do not explain the MRI pattern or ophthalmoplegia as well.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
The alternatives are excluded by the imaging topography plus documented severe thiamine deficiency in a clear malnutrition setting.
I commit to Wernicke encephalopathy. Vitamin A deficiency may contribute to the vision loss.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Non-alcoholic Wernicke encephalopathy (severe thiamine deficiency from recurrent vomiting/malnutrition) with optic neuropathy, hearing loss and polyneuropathy.

CASE: M078

KEY FINDINGS:
Paternal family history of multiple hereditary exostoses (HME) and multiple sessile exostoses (pelvis, lower limbs). This confirms HME, which carries a risk of malignant transformation.
Progressive, activity-related pain and growing deformity.
The fibular lesion has irregular borders, heterogeneous matrix, a gadolinium-enhancing cap, heterogeneous T2 signal and soft-tissue infiltration. These are red flags for malignant transformation.
Biopsy shows malignant mesenchymal proliferation with atypia and spindle cells.
Normal inflammatory and metabolic labs argue against infection or a metabolic cause.
Note: symptoms are reported in the left leg while the suspicious lesion is in the right fibula. This discrepancy should be clarified.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma arising in an osteochondroma (HME)
Dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma
Secondary osteosarcoma arising in an exostosis
Benign symptomatic osteochondroma (bursitis, fracture, nerve/vessel impingement)
Ewing sarcoma or other primary bone sarcoma
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma
For: HME, which is the most common malignant transformation; cap enhancement; irregular margins; soft-tissue invasion; biopsy shows malignancy.
Against: age 8 is unusually young; the 6-mm cap is thin; spindle cells are not typical of low-grade chondrosarcoma.
Dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma
For: spindle-cell component.
Against: very rare in children.
Secondary osteosarcoma
For: child, spindle cells.
Against: no osteoid production is described; arising from an exostosis is exceptional.
Benign osteochondroma
For: HME commonly causes pain.
Against: histology shows malignancy and there is soft-tissue infiltration.
Ewing sarcoma
For: age.
Against: spindle cells rather than small round cells; arises in the setting of an exostosis.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
A benign lesion is excluded by histology.
Ewing sarcoma is excluded by morphology.
Osteosarcoma and dedifferentiated tumor are less likely without osteoid or a biphasic description.
I commit to malignant transformation to secondary chondrosarcoma.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Malignant transformation of an osteochondroma in multiple hereditary exostoses: secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma of the fibula.

CASE: M079

KEY FINDINGS:
Lifelong recurrent right upper quadrant pain from adolescence, suggesting a congenital lesion.
Multifocal saccular and fusiform intrahepatic bile duct dilations with intrahepatic stones and a "central dot sign" (portal radicles within dilated ducts). This sign is pathognomonic.
No hepatomegaly, normal liver function and blood count, and no features of portal hypertension. This argues against congenital hepatic fibrosis.
Rural dairy farming raises a parasitic consideration.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Caroli disease (Todani type V choledochal cyst) with hepatolithiasis
Caroli syndrome (with congenital hepatic fibrosis)
Recurrent pyogenic cholangitis / primary hepatolithiasis
Parasitic cholangiopathy (Fasciola hepatica)
Primary sclerosing cholangitis or polycystic liver disease
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Caroli disease
For: central dot sign, saccular communicating ductal ectasia, intrahepatic stones, early onset.
Against: none.
Caroli syndrome
For: same ductal findings.
Against: no hepatomegaly or portal hypertension, normal platelets.
Recurrent pyogenic cholangitis
For: intrahepatic stones.
Against: no cholangitis episodes or fever; the central dot sign is not explained.
Fasciola
For: livestock exposure.
Against: normal eosinophils and blood count, no parasitic findings, congenital-type ectasia.
PSC or polycystic disease
Against: PSC shows strictures and beading rather than saccular ectasia. Polycystic liver cysts do not communicate with the ducts and show no central dot.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Caroli syndrome is excluded by the absence of fibrosis/portal hypertension.
Pyogenic and parasitic causes are excluded by the absent clinical and lab features.
PSC and polycystic disease are excluded by imaging morphology.
I commit to Caroli disease.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Caroli disease (Todani type V, congenital intrahepatic bile duct ectasia) with hepatolithiasis.

CASE: M080

KEY FINDINGS:
Very high ASO titer (1,450 IU/mL), showing recent streptococcal infection.
Carditis: new aortic and mitral regurgitation, reduced LV function and a conduction disturbance (Mobitz II AV block, bradycardia, exertional palpitations).
Markedly raised ESR (70) and CRP (20.2), plus mild anemia.
Right iliac fossa pain with vomiting and anorexia, but a 5-mm appendix and no peritonism. This is a recognized abdominal pain presentation of rheumatic fever that mimics appendicitis.
Negative blood and urine cultures.
DIFFERENTIAL:
Acute rheumatic fever with carditis
Infective endocarditis
Viral/infectious myocarditis (including Lyme carditis)
Acute appendicitis or urinary tract infection
MIS-C / Kawasaki disease
EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:
Acute rheumatic fever
For: preceding streptococcal evidence, valvulitis of both aortic and mitral valves, AV block, high ESR/CRP, abdominal pain. This meets Jones criteria as carditis (major) plus elevated acute-phase reactants and conduction abnormality (minor).
Against: no arthritis, chorea or fever documented.
Infective endocarditis
For: regurgitation, high inflammatory markers.
Against: negative cultures, no vegetations described.
Myocarditis / Lyme
For: LV dysfunction and heart block.
Against: valvular regurgitation and high ASO point to rheumatic disease; no tick exposure or rash.
Appendicitis or UTI
For: right iliac fossa pain, dysuria.
Against: normal appendix, no rebound, negative urine culture, no explanation for the cardiac findings.
MIS-C / Kawasaki
For: inflammation, cardiac involvement, gastrointestinal symptoms.
Against: age, no rash, conjunctivitis or mucosal changes, no coronary findings, strep evidence.
EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:
Endocarditis is excluded by negative cultures.
Appendicitis and UTI are excluded by ultrasound and culture.
Myocarditis and MIS-C are less consistent with valvulitis plus high ASO.
I commit to acute rheumatic fever. The abdominal pain mimicking appendicitis and the high-grade AV block are atypical but documented features.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Acute rheumatic fever with carditis (aortic and mitral valvulitis, Mobitz II AV block), presenting with pseudo-appendicular abdominal pain.
