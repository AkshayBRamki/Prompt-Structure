CASE: M073
REASONING:

The patient has long-standing heavy alcohol use and severe malnutrition, with low folate (B9), macrocytosis and elevated liver enzymes.
He developed confusion, stupor, dysarthria and comprehension problems over 10 days. He also has apraxia, astasia-abasia, frontal release signs (grasp reflex, perseveration), spasticity and incontinence. Together these suggest interhemispheric disconnection plus frontal involvement.
Imaging shows symmetric T2/FLAIR and DWI hyperintensity of the entire corpus callosum, with low ADC indicating cytotoxic edema. The splenium is hypodense on CT. MR angiography is normal, which argues against a vascular cause.
The bilateral frontal cortical hyperintensity is consistent with associated cortical laminar necrosis (Morel's laminar sclerosis).
This pattern in a malnourished alcoholic is classic for demyelination and necrosis of the corpus callosum.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Marchiafava-Bignami disease (acute form, with frontal cortical involvement)

CASE: M074
REASONING:

A young woman had a 4.7 cm renal tumor called "papillary RCC" on morphology alone, with no immunohistochemistry.
Fifteen years after nephrectomy, she has retroperitoneal soft-tissue masses with mild enhancement and cystic change.
Very late recurrence, young age, female sex and papillary-appearing histology misclassified without IHC are characteristic of MiT family translocation RCC. These tumors often look papillary and are known for late metastases to lymph nodes and the retroperitoneum.
The confirmatory IHC (TFE3) would establish the diagnosis.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Xp11.2 (TFE3) translocation renal cell carcinoma with late retroperitoneal recurrence/metastasis, originally misdiagnosed as papillary RCC

CASE: M075
REASONING:

She has an acute, paroxysmal dry cough lasting one week. The cough is severe enough to stop her from completing a 6-minute walk, which suggests exertion triggers it.
Her background points to airway hyperresponsiveness: lifelong atopy (allergic rhinitis) and occupational wood-dust exposure.
The workup is normal: chest X-ray, HRCT, echocardiogram, blood eosinophils and spirometry. This excludes interstitial lung disease (including rituximab-induced pneumonitis), infection with infiltrates, cardiac causes and classic obstructive asthma.
She is immunosuppressed with steroids and rituximab, but she is afebrile, her oxygen saturation is normal and her imaging is clean, which makes opportunistic pneumonia unlikely.
An isolated cough with normal spirometry in an atopic patient exposed to wood dust fits cough-variant asthma. It would be confirmed by bronchial provocation (methacholine) testing. Non-asthmatic eosinophilic bronchitis is the main alternative.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Cough-variant asthma (likely allergic/occupational, wood dust–related)

CASE: M076
REASONING:

She had a month of parenteral nutrition, presumably without adequate thiamine supplementation.
She has the classic triad: ophthalmoplegia (multidirectional gaze limitation and diplopia), ataxia (unsteady gait, positive Romberg) and altered mentation (slowed comprehension). She also has hiccups and miosis, consistent with brainstem and periaqueductal involvement.
Her vitamin B1 level is low.
MRI shows bilateral thalamic and periaqueductal lesions, which is the classic distribution. Her earlier MRI was normal, and the lesions improved on follow-up, consistent with treatment.
A normal AQP-4 antibody excludes NMOSD, and the absence of albuminocytologic dissociation argues against Miller Fisher/GBS.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Wernicke encephalopathy due to thiamine deficiency during parenteral nutrition

CASE: M077
REASONING:

She had four months of recurrent vomiting, dysphagia and a 60-lb weight loss, causing severe malnutrition with multiple vitamin deficiencies. Her thiamine is undetectably low.
She has ophthalmoplegia (esotropia, nystagmus, diplopia), ataxic wide-based gait and areflexia with numbness (concurrent dry-beriberi neuropathy). She also has severe vision and hearing loss, which are recognized atypical features of severe thiamine deficiency.
MRI shows T2 hyperintensity in the medial thalami and periaqueductal region, which is the classic finding.
There is no alcohol use, so this is non-alcoholic Wernicke secondary to GI losses and malnutrition.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Non-alcoholic Wernicke encephalopathy (thiamine deficiency) secondary to prolonged vomiting and malnutrition

CASE: M078
REASONING:

The child has a family history of hereditary multiple exostoses, and CT shows multiple exostoses in the pelvis and limbs. This establishes HME.
He has a progressive, activity-related painful lesion with concerning features in the fibula: irregular borders, a thickened and enhancing cartilaginous cap, heterogeneous signal and soft-tissue infiltration.
The biopsy shows a malignant mesenchymal tumor with atypia.
Malignant transformation of an osteochondroma in HME is most commonly to a secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma.
The case is internally inconsistent: the symptoms are in the left leg but the imaging describes the right fibula. This does not change the diagnosis.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Secondary peripheral chondrosarcoma arising from an osteochondroma (malignant transformation) in hereditary multiple exostoses

CASE: M079
REASONING:

A young man has recurrent RUQ pain since adolescence, with normal liver tests and no cholangitis at presentation.
Imaging shows multifocal saccular and fusiform dilation of the intrahepatic ducts with intraductal stones and no gallbladder stones.
MRCP shows the "central dot sign" (portal radicles surrounded by dilated ducts), which is pathognomonic for congenital cystic dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts.
There is no evidence of portal hypertension or hepatic fibrosis, so this is the pure form rather than the syndrome.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Caroli disease with intrahepatic lithiasis (hepatolithiasis)

CASE: M080
REASONING:

There is evidence of recent streptococcal infection: ASO is markedly elevated at 1,450 IU/mL.
Inflammatory markers are high: ESR 70 mm/h and CRP 20.2 mg/dL. He also has anemia of inflammation.
He has carditis: new aortic and mitral regurgitation, mildly reduced LV function, and a conduction disturbance (second-degree AV block with bradycardia and palpitations).
The right iliac fossa pain mimics appendicitis, but the appendix is normal at 5 mm, consistent with the recognized abdominal pain of rheumatic fever.
Negative blood and urine cultures argue against endocarditis and pyelonephritis.
Major (carditis) and minor (elevated ESR/CRP, conduction abnormality) Jones criteria are met, with evidence of prior streptococcal infection.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Acute rheumatic fever with rheumatic carditis (valvulitis and AV conduction block), with abdominal pain mimicking appendicitis
