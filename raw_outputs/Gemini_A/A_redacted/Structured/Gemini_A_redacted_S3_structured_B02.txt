CASE: M009

KEY FINDINGS:

Demographics and presentation: 13-year-old boy with a painless, slow-growing, spherical, bony-hard mass on the buccal aspect of the right maxillary alveolar ridge (first premolar to first molar) covered by intact, tense, pale mucosa; adjacent teeth have normal pulp vitality. Significance: Points toward a benign, non-odontogenic, surface osseous lesion.

Negative history: No trauma, parafunctional habits (bruxism/clenching), or family history of Gardner syndrome. Significance: Argues against reactive periostitis or syndromic multiple osteomas.

CBCT findings: Exophytic, mushroom-like, radiodense mass showing direct continuity with the underlying cortical and trabecular bone. Significance: Characteristic imaging hallmark of a benign exostosis or osteochondroma rather than an encapsulated or destructive neoplasm.

Histopathology: Bony trabeculae separated by connective tissue, consistent with benign exostosis/hyperostosis. Significance: Confirms a benign mature osseous proliferation and rules out malignancy or cartilaginous neoplasia.

DIFFERENTIAL:

Maxillary buccal exostosis (alveolar exostosis)

Peripheral (periosteal) osteoma

Osteochondroma

Ossifying fibroma (peripheral)

EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:

Maxillary buccal exostosis:

For: Classic location on the buccal aspect of the maxillary alveolar ridge; painless, bony-hard, well-demarcated enlargement with intact overlying mucosa; vital adjacent teeth; CBCT showing exophytic radiodense growth continuous with cortical and trabecular bone; histopathology confirming benign exostosis/hyperostosis.

Against: Typically presents in adults and is often bilateral/multiple, whereas this patient is 13 years old with a solitary lesion; no reported bruxism or occlusal trauma.

Peripheral osteoma:

For: Painless, solitary, bony-hard, well-circumscribed exophytic radiodense mass on the maxilla; histologically composed of mature bone.

Against: Less commonly arises on the buccal alveolar ridge compared to the mandibular angle or paranasal sinuses; no family history of Gardner syndrome; histopathology specifically favors benign exostosis/hyperostosis.

Osteochondroma:

For: Mushroom-like exophytic bony lesion with continuity to both cortical and trabecular bone on CBCT; occurs in adolescents.

Against: Extremely rare in the maxillary alveolar ridge (typically affects the mandibular condyle or coronoid process); histopathology shows only bony trabeculae and connective tissue without the diagnostic hyaline cartilage cap.

Peripheral ossifying fibroma:

For: Occurs on the maxillary gingiva/alveolar ridge in adolescents and young adults.

Against: Typically presents as a soft-to-firm gingival mass rather than a bony-hard mass continuous with cortical/trabecular bone on CBCT; histopathology lacks cellular fibrous stroma with variable calcifications.

EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:

Osteochondroma is excluded due to the absence of a cartilaginous cap on histopathology.

Peripheral ossifying fibroma is excluded by the bony-hard consistency, direct continuity with cortical and trabecular bone on CBCT, and mature osseous histopathology.

Peripheral osteoma is less favored given the classic buccal alveolar ridge location, absence of Gardner syndrome, and histopathological confirmation of benign exostosis/hyperostosis.

Commitment: The combination of classic buccal maxillary alveolar location, CBCT continuity with cortical/trabecular bone, vital adjacent teeth, and confirmatory histopathology establishes maxillary buccal exostosis.

FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Maxillary buccal exostosis

CASE: M010

KEY FINDINGS:

History of uterine trauma: 34-year-old woman presenting 2 months after dilation and curettage (D&C) for a 15-week missed abortion. Significance: Uterine instrumentation is the primary risk factor for acquired uterine vascular anomalies.

Severe hemorrhage with hemodynamic instability: Menorrhagia with clots, severe cramping, fatigue, confusion, and presyncope. Significance: Classic presentation of high-flow arterial uterine bleeding rather than routine post-abortal spotting.

TVUS and MRI findings: Echogenic mass with markedly increased blood flow on TVUS; mixed endometrial mass with serpiginous vessel flow voids and solid enhancement on MRI. Significance: Narrows the differential to hypervascular retained products of conception (RPOC), gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD), or a vascular malformation.

CTA and selective angiography: Cluster of vessels with early venous filling on CTA, supplied by the left uterine artery on selective angiography. Significance: Early venous filling (arteriovenous shunting) is pathognomonic for an arteriovenous malformation.

DIFFERENTIAL:

Acquired uterine arteriovenous malformation (AVM)

Retained products of conception (RPOC) with enhanced myometrial vascularity

Gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN)

Uterine artery pseudoaneurysm

EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:

Acquired uterine arteriovenous malformation (AVM):

For: Recent D&C (uterine trauma); delayed, torrential abnormal uterine bleeding causing hemodynamic instability; serpiginous vessels on MRI; CTA showing a cluster of vessels with early venous filling; selective angiography confirming arterial supply from the left uterine artery.

Against: Initial ultrasound and MRI showed an echogenic/solid endometrial mass component, which can mimic RPOC.

Retained products of conception (RPOC):

For: Recent missed abortion and D&C; echogenic endometrial mass with increased vascularity on TVUS and solid enhancement on MRI.

Against: Does not explain the classic vascular nidus with early venous filling (AV shunting) confirmed on CTA and angiography.

Gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN):

For: Post-pregnancy abnormal bleeding and hypervascular uterine mass on TVUS/MRI.

Against: Prior pregnancy was a documented missed abortion rather than a molar pregnancy, no hCG elevation is reported, and CTA/angiography specifically confirms a vascular cluster with early venous filling rather than a malignant trophoblastic tumor.

Uterine artery pseudoaneurysm:

For: Occurs after D&C and causes severe delayed hemorrhage.

Against: Typically appears as a single saccular outpouching with turbulent ("yin-yang") flow rather than a cluster of serpiginous vessels with early venous filling.

EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:

Uterine artery pseudoaneurysm is excluded by the presence of a vessel cluster and early venous filling rather than a focal saccular arterial dilatation.

GTN is excluded by the lack of supporting clinical/biochemical features and definitive angiographic demonstration of an arteriovenous shunt.

RPOC (though initially suspected and sometimes coexistent) cannot account for the definitive arteriovenous shunting seen on CTA and selective angiography.

Commitment: Post-D&C torrential hemorrhage combined with serpiginous vessels on MRI and early venous filling supplied by the left uterine artery on CTA/angiography is diagnostic of an acquired uterine arteriovenous malformation (AVM).

FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Acquired uterine arteriovenous malformation (AVM)

CASE: M011

KEY FINDINGS:

High-risk host and constitutional symptoms: 33-year-old male with history of IV drug use, newly diagnosed HIV, fever (38.4°C), anorexia, and recent weight loss. Significance: Indicates an immunocompromised state predisposing to opportunistic and disseminated infections.

Intestinal tuberculosis (cecal perforation due to disseminated tuberculosis in HIV)

Acute perforated appendicitis

Crohn's disease with perforation

Cecal adenocarcinoma or HIV-associated intestinal lymphoma with perforation

Intestinal tuberculosis (disseminated tuberculosis in HIV):

For: Newly diagnosed HIV and constitutional symptoms (fever, weight loss, anemia); concurrent pulmonary TB findings (RUL consolidation, tree-in-bud bronchiolitis); perforated cecal mass with diffuse white serosal/intestinal lesions (tubercles); necrotizing granulomatous colitis/lymphadenitis; positive MTB PCR.

For: Weight loss, anemia, HIV status, and perforated cecal mass.

Against: Histopathology showed necrotizing granulomatous inflammation rather than malignant cells, and MTB PCR was positive.

EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:

Acute appendicitis is excluded by surgical and pathological findings.

Crohn's disease and gastrointestinal malignancy/lymphoma are excluded by necrotizing granulomatous histopathology, concomitant pulmonary tree-in-bud lesions, and positive PCR for Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Commitment: The positive MTB PCR, necrotizing granulomatous colitis with cecal perforation, pulmonary TB imaging, and new HIV diagnosis definitively establish intestinal tuberculosis with cecal perforation (disseminated tuberculosis in HIV).

FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Intestinal tuberculosis with cecal perforation (disseminated tuberculosis in newly diagnosed HIV)

CASE: M012

KEY FINDINGS:

Acute hemorrhagic shock: 84-year-old hospitalized male with sudden, severe, diffuse abdominal pain, hypotension, tachycardia, and an acute hemoglobin drop from 13.1 g/dL to 8.8 g/dL in 24 hours. Significance: Indicates catastrophic acute intra-abdominal hemorrhage.

Atraumatic (spontaneous) splenic rupture secondary to a benign subcapsular splenic lesion (e.g., splenic hemangioma)

Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)

Spontaneous splenic rupture secondary to splenic malignancy (e.g., angiosarcoma or lymphoma)

Spontaneous splenic rupture secondary to infectious splenomegaly/abscess

EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:

Atraumatic (spontaneous) splenic rupture secondary to a benign subcapsular splenic lesion (e.g., splenic hemangioma):

For: Acute diffuse abdominal pain, hemorrhagic shock, rapid 4.3 g/dL Hb drop, hemoperitoneum with active bleeding on CTA requiring emergency splenectomy, and a 1 cm benign subcapsular nodule on histopathology without malignancy or infection (hemangioma is the most common benign splenic tumor capable of causing spontaneous rupture, potentially precipitated by coughing from pneumonia).

Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA):

For: 84-year-old male with hypertension, sudden severe abdominal pain, hypotension, tachycardia, and acute hemoglobin drop.

Against: Excluded by CTA findings and the fact that the patient underwent splenectomy rather than aortic repair.

For: Advanced age (84) and presence of a 1 cm subcapsular splenic nodule causing spontaneous rupture.

Splenic rupture secondary to infection:

For: Concurrent hospitalization for bacterial pneumonia.

Against: Explicitly excluded by postsplenectomy histopathology showing no infectious organisms.

EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:

Ruptured AAA is excluded by CTA and the surgical intervention (splenectomy).

Malignant and infectious causes of splenic rupture are directly excluded by postsplenectomy histopathology.

Commitment: The acute hemoperitoneum with active bleeding requiring splenectomy in an inpatient without trauma, coupled with a benign 1 cm subcapsular nodule, establishes atraumatic (spontaneous) splenic rupture (secondary to a benign subcapsular splenic lesion, most likely a splenic hemangioma).

FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Atraumatic (spontaneous) splenic rupture secondary to a benign subcapsular splenic lesion (splenic hemangioma)

CASE: M013

KEY FINDINGS:

Chief complaint and etiology: 22-year-old male with upper anterior spacing and forwardly placed upper front teeth, with a history of childhood thumb-sucking. Significance: Classic habit-induced dentoalveolar malocclusion causing proclination of maxillary incisors and anterior spacing.

Extraoral features: Convex facial profile, acute nasolabial angle, deep mentolabial sulcus, increased overjet and overbite, and lip incompetency with a lower lip trap. Significance: Strongly indicates maxillary incisor proclination/protrusion with secondary soft-tissue lip entrapment that perpetuates the overjet.

Intraoral features: Class I posterior buccal occlusion, generalized upper anterior spacing, lower anterior crowding, maintained midline, and high labial frenal attachment. Significance: Defines an Angle's Class I molar relationship with anterior dentoalveolar discrepancies (proclination/spacing in maxilla, crowding in mandible).

Radiographic/cephalometric findings: Class I skeletal relationship. Significance: Confirms the malocclusion is dentoalveolar rather than a skeletal Class II jaw discrepancy.

DIFFERENTIAL:

Angle's Class I malocclusion with maxillary anterior proclination/spacing and mandibular anterior crowding (Dewey's Type 1 and Type 2)

Skeletal Class II division 1 malocclusion

Bimaxillary dentoalveolar protrusion (Class I)

Angle's Class II division 1 dental malocclusion

EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:

Angle's Class I malocclusion with maxillary anterior proclination/spacing and lower crowding:

For: Confirmed Class I skeletal relationship on cephalometry; Class I posterior buccal occlusion; forwardly placed upper incisors with increased overjet, acute nasolabial angle, convex profile, and lower lip trap; childhood thumb-sucking history; upper anterior spacing and lower anterior crowding.

Against: None; explains every extraoral, intraoral, and radiographic feature.

Skeletal Class II division 1 malocclusion:

For: Convex facial profile, increased overjet, deep mentolabial sulcus, lip incompetency, and lower lip trap.

Against: Directly excluded by cephalometric analysis confirming a Class I skeletal relationship and Class I posterior occlusion.

Bimaxillary dentoalveolar protrusion:

For: Class I skeletal and molar relationship, convex profile, acute nasolabial angle, and lip incompetency.

Against: The patient specifically has crowding in the lower anterior region and a deep mentolabial sulcus with a lower lip trap (which typically retroclines or crowds lower incisors rather than protruding them alongside upper incisors).

Angle's Class II division 1 dental malocclusion:

For: Proclined maxillary incisors, increased overjet, and lower lip trap.

Against: Excluded by the documented proper Class I posterior buccal occlusion.

EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:

Skeletal Class II and dental Class II division 1 malocclusions are excluded by the Class I skeletal relationship on cephalometry and Class I posterior buccal occlusion.

Pure bimaxillary protrusion is less consistent with the deep mentolabial sulcus, lower lip trap, and mandibular anterior crowding.

Commitment: The Class I skeletal and molar relationships combined with thumb-sucking-induced proclined/spaced maxillary incisors, increased overjet, and lower anterior crowding establish Angle's Class I malocclusion with proclined/spaced maxillary incisors (maxillary dentoalveolar protrusion) and mandibular anterior crowding.

FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Angle's Class I malocclusion with proclined and spaced maxillary anterior teeth (Dewey's modification)

CASE: M014

KEY FINDINGS:

Clinical presentation: 24-year-old female with chronic urinary symptoms (dysuria, pollakiuria) since puberty and progressively worsening lower back pain (lumbago) over the past year, without radiculopathy. Significance: Progressive neurogenic bladder symptoms coupled with worsening lumbago in a young adult strongly suggest an occult spinal dysraphism / cord tethering.

Normal urologic and laboratory workup: Normal WBC, hemoglobin, creatinine, urinalysis (no infection), and normal renovesical ultrasound. Significance: Rules out urinary tract infection, nephrolithiasis, or structural renal/bladder pathology as the cause of chronic urinary symptoms.

Lumbar MRI: Low-lying conus medullaris at S1–S2 tethered to a sacral spinal canal lipoma, associated with hypoplastic sacral vertebrae, anterior vertebral body scalloping, and L4–L5 spondylolisthesis with bilateral isthmic lysis. Significance: Diagnostic of adult tethered cord syndrome secondary to a congenital sacral spinal lipoma (occult spinal dysraphism).

DIFFERENTIAL:

Adult tethered cord syndrome secondary to a sacral spinal lipoma (occult spinal dysraphism)

Symptomatic L4–L5 isthmic spondylolisthesis

Interstitial cystitis / bladder pain syndrome

Cauda equina syndrome (secondary to disc herniation or neoplasm)

EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:

Adult tethered cord syndrome secondary to a sacral spinal lipoma:

For: Chronic neurogenic bladder symptoms (pollakiuria, dysuria) beginning around puberty (growth spurt increases cord tension); progressive non-radiating low back pain; normal urinalysis and renovesical ultrasound; MRI directly demonstrating a low-lying conus medullaris at S1–S2 tethered to a sacral spinal canal lipoma with associated sacral hypoplasia and vertebral scalloping.

Against: Normal neurological physical examination (though adult tethered cord frequently presents with isolated pain and urologic dysfunction without overt motor/sensory deficits).

Symptomatic L4–L5 isthmic spondylolisthesis:

For: Present on lumbar MRI and can contribute to chronic low back pain.

Against: Does not explain chronic urinary symptoms since puberty, the low-lying conus at S1–S2, the sacral lipoma, or sacral hypoplasia.

Interstitial cystitis / bladder pain syndrome:

For: Young adult female with chronic dysuria and urinary frequency, negative urinalysis, and normal renovesical ultrasound.

Against: Does not explain the worsening lumbago or the definitive congenital spinal cord tethering seen on MRI.

Cauda equina syndrome:

For: Low back pain with urinary dysfunction.

Against: Chronic, indolent course since puberty rather than acute/subacute compression; normal physical exam (no saddle anesthesia or lower extremity weakness); MRI shows congenital tethering by a lipoma rather than acute cauda equina compression.

EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:

Interstitial cystitis and cauda equina syndrome are excluded by the chronic congenital presentation and definitive MRI findings.

L4–L5 isthmic spondylolisthesis is a coexisting structural finding that may contribute to lumbago, but it cannot account for the primary neurogenic bladder dysfunction or conus tethering.

Commitment: The combination of chronic unexplained urologic dysfunction since puberty, progressive lumbago, and MRI confirmation of a conus medullaris tethered at S1–S2 to a sacral spinal lipoma definitively establishes adult tethered cord syndrome secondary to a sacral spinal lipoma.

FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Adult tethered cord syndrome secondary to a sacral spinal lipoma

CASE: M015

KEY FINDINGS:

Progressive pure cerebellar syndrome: 41-year-old male with 2 years of progressive gait imbalance and 1 year of scanning speech; examination reveals pure pancerebellar signs (broad-based gait, dysmetria, dyssynergia, dysdiadochokinesia, positive rebound, jerky horizontal nystagmus, hypotonia, pendular reflexes) with intact cognition, normal 5/5 strength, and intact sensory modalities (touch, pain, vibration, proprioception). Significance: Localizes strictly to the cerebellum without corticospinal, dorsal column, or peripheral nerve involvement.

Autosomal recessive inheritance pattern: Two siblings have similar gait issues, while parents and a sister's children are unaffected. Significance: Strongly favors an autosomal recessive hereditary ataxia over an autosomal dominant or sporadic disorder.

Negative acquired triggers: No alcohol use, smoking, toxin exposure, or long-term medications. Significance: Rules out the most common acquired toxic/nutritional causes of cerebellar degeneration.

Neuroimaging: CT scan demonstrates marked cerebellar atrophy. Significance: Confirms chronic neurodegenerative cerebellar loss.

DIFFERENTIAL:

Autosomal recessive hereditary cerebellar ataxia (adult-onset / Holmes-type hereditary cerebellar ataxia)

Spinocerebellar ataxia (SCA, autosomal dominant)

Friedreich ataxia

Multiple system atrophy, cerebellar type (MSA-C)

EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:

Autosomal recessive hereditary cerebellar ataxia (adult-onset / Holmes-type):

For: Adult onset (age 39) of slowly progressive pure cerebellar ataxia; affected siblings with unaffected parents and offspring (classic autosomal recessive pedigree); intact sensation and cognition; marked cerebellar atrophy on CT; absence of toxins/alcohol.

Against: Specific genetic mutation is not provided (clinical-radiologic-pedigree diagnosis).

Spinocerebellar ataxia (SCA, autosomal dominant):

For: Adult-onset progressive cerebellar ataxia with marked cerebellar atrophy on CT and positive family history.

Against: Unaffected parents with multiple affected siblings strongly argues against autosomal dominant transmission (unless incomplete penetrance or non-paternity is invoked).

Friedreich ataxia:

For: Most common autosomal recessive hereditary ataxia, fitting the affected-siblings/unaffected-parents pedigree.

Against: Typically presents before age 25; classically features dorsal column sensory loss (loss of vibration/proprioception), areflexia, extensor plantar responses, and cardiomyopathy/skeletal deformities, with relatively mild early cerebellar atrophy—whereas this patient has intact proprioception/vibration, pendular reflexes, age 39 onset, and marked cerebellar atrophy.

Multiple system atrophy, cerebellar type (MSA-C):

For: Adult-onset progressive cerebellar ataxia with cerebellar atrophy.

Against: Almost exclusively sporadic (contradicted by two affected siblings) and typically accompanied by autonomic dysfunction or parkinsonism, which are absent.

EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:

MSA-C and acquired toxic/alcoholic degeneration are excluded by the negative exposure history and strong familial clustering in siblings.

Friedreich ataxia is excluded by the late adult onset, completely intact proprioception and vibration sense, preserved (pendular) reflexes, and marked cerebellar atrophy.

Autosomal dominant SCA is less favored given unaffected parents and offspring across generations.

Commitment: Adult-onset pure cerebellar degeneration with marked cerebellar atrophy on CT and a clear autosomal recessive family history establishes autosomal recessive hereditary cerebellar ataxia (late-onset / Holmes-type cerebellar ataxia).

FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Autosomal recessive hereditary cerebellar ataxia (late-onset / Holmes-type hereditary cerebellar ataxia)

CASE: M016

KEY FINDINGS:

Rapidly progressive neurological decline: 66-year-old woman with subacute cognitive decline, confusion, hallucinations, expressive aphasia, progressive weakness, intermittent fevers, and SIADH leading to death within 4 months despite treatment. Significance: Indicates an aggressive, diffuse CNS infiltrative or neoplastic process.

CSF biomarker: CSF pleocytosis with elevated IL-10, alongside negative infectious, autoimmune, metabolic, and paraneoplastic panels. Significance: Elevated CSF IL-10 is a highly specific biomarker for primary CNS B-cell lymphoma.

Characteristic neuroimaging evolution: Initial MRI showed diffuse, non-enhancing T2/FLAIR hyperintensities without mass effect or restricted diffusion; follow-up MRI showed new asymmetric diffusion restriction and hyperperfusion in the caudate and hippocampus; MR spectroscopy showed elevated Cho:Cr and decreased NAA. Significance: Diffuse non-enhancing leukoencephalopathy with neoplastic spectroscopy (high Cho:Cr, low NAA) is the hallmark of lymphomatosis cerebri, while subsequent focal diffusion restriction indicates superimposed ischemic infarction or focal high-grade lymphomatous infiltration.

Autopsy confirmation: Infiltration by CD20-positive B cells with superimposed focal lesions in the caudate and hippocampus. Significance: Pathologically confirms a diffuse B-cell CNS lymphoma (lymphomatosis cerebri).

DIFFERENTIAL:

Lymphomatosis cerebri (diffuse variant of primary CNS diffuse large B-cell lymphoma)

Intravascular large B-cell lymphoma (IVLBCL)

Gliomatosis cerebri (diffuse infiltrating glioma)

Autoimmune or infectious encephalitis (e.g., viral, prion disease / CJD)

EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:

Lymphomatosis cerebri (primary CNS diffuse large B-cell lymphoma):

For: Subacute rapidly progressive dementia, aphasia, weakness, and fatal course; elevated CSF IL-10 and pleocytosis; classic initial MRI of diffuse, non-enhancing T2/FLAIR hyperintensities without mass effect; hypercellular/neoplastic MR spectroscopy (elevated Cho:Cr, decreased NAA); autopsy confirming CD20-positive B-cell infiltration (with superimposed focal infarcts/lymphomatous transformation in the caudate and hippocampus).

Against: None; accounts for the entire clinical, biochemical, radiologic, and autopsy profile.

Intravascular large B-cell lymphoma (IVLBCL):

For: CD20-positive B cells, subacute encephalopathy, intermittent fevers, SIADH, and multifocal diffusion restriction (infarcts) on follow-up MRI.

Against: Typically presents early with multifocal acute/subacute infarcts and patchy enhancement rather than initial diffuse, non-enhancing T2/FLAIR white matter hyperintensities without restricted diffusion; however, it remains the closest B-cell mimic.

Gliomatosis cerebri:

For: Diffuse, non-enhancing T2/FLAIR hyperintensities without discrete mass effect and elevated Cho:Cr with decreased NAA on MR spectroscopy.

Against: Excluded by elevated CSF IL-10 and autopsy demonstrating CD20-positive B cells rather than glial tumor cells.

Autoimmune or infectious encephalitis / CJD:

For: Rapidly progressive dementia, hallucinations, aphasia, CSF pleocytosis, and asymmetric caudate/hippocampal diffusion restriction.

Against: Broad infectious, autoimmune, and paraneoplastic workups were negative; MR spectroscopy indicated a hypercellular neoplasm; autopsy confirmed CD20-positive B-cell lymphoma.

EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:

Autoimmune/infectious encephalitis and prion disease are excluded by negative panels, neoplastic MR spectroscopy, elevated CSF IL-10, and CD20+ B-cell pathology.

Gliomatosis cerebri is excluded by elevated CSF IL-10 and CD20+ B-cell autopsy confirmation.

IVLBCL is less favored than lymphomatosis cerebri given the initial diffuse, non-enhancing T2/FLAIR hyperintensities without restricted diffusion or mass effect.

Commitment: The combination of subacute fatal cognitive decline, elevated CSF IL-10, initial diffuse non-enhancing T2/FLAIR hyperintensities with hypercellular MR spectroscopy, and autopsy-proven CD20-positive B-cell infiltration establishes lymphomatosis cerebri (primary CNS diffuse large B-cell lymphoma) with superimposed focal ischemia/infarction (or focal lymphomatous lesions) in the caudate and hippocampus.
