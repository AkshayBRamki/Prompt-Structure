KEY FINDINGS:

DIFFERENTIAL:

Infected urachal cyst (urachal abscess)

Urachal carcinoma (with secondary infection/necrosis)

Abdominal wall abscess / stitch granuloma / gossypiboma secondary to prior caesarean section

Abdominal wall endometriosis (scar endometrioma) with secondary infection

Infected urachal cyst (urachal abscess):

For: Classic anatomical path on CT connecting the umbilicus to the superoanterior bladder wall; young adult age (27 years); fever, leukocytosis (16,880 cells/mL), spontaneous rupture discharging pus and blood, and CT features explicitly indicating abscess formation extending into the lower abdominal wall musculature and subcutis.

Urachal carcinoma (with secondary infection/necrosis):

For: Anatomical location along the urachus (umbilicus to superoanterior bladder dome); semi-solid mass with vascularized edges on ultrasound; 7 kg weight loss and decreased appetite over 3 months.

Against: Patient is very young (27 years; urachal carcinoma typically occurs in older adults, predominantly men); prominent purulent rupture, fever, leukocytosis, and CT findings specifically characterizing an abscess extending into the abdominal wall strongly favor a primary infectious etiology.

For: Reproductive-age woman with prior caesarean section and a lower abdominal wall mass.

Against: No cyclical pain linked to menstruation reported; does not explain the anatomical tract linking the umbilicus to the superoanterior bladder wall; overt purulent rupture and marked leukocytosis point to a primary abscess.

EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:

Post-caesarean abdominal wall abscess/gossypiboma and scar endometrioma are excluded because they do not account for the defining CT finding of a lesion spanning from the umbilicus to the superoanterior bladder wall.

Urachal carcinoma is less likely given the patient's young age (27 years), frank purulent rupture, leukocytosis, and CT morphology favoring abscess formation, although histopathology is required if a residual mass persists after drainage.

Committing to infected urachal cyst (urachal abscess) as it uniquely integrates the exact urachal anatomy on CT with the acute-on-chronic suppurative clinical presentation.

FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Infected urachal cyst (urachal abscess)

Sodium channel myotonias and muscular dystrophies are excluded by the warm-up phenomenon, normal blood work, and definitive identification of a truncating skeletal muscle chloride channel (CLCN1) mutation.

FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Autosomal recessive myotonia congenita (CLCN1-related)

CASE: M068

KEY FINDINGS:

Hereditary spherocytosis

Hereditary elliptocytosis / hereditary pyropoikilocytosis

Hereditary xerocytosis (dehydrated hereditary stomatocytosis)

Crigler-Najjar syndrome

Hereditary spherocytosis:

For: Classic cause of severe, early-onset (first 24 hours) non-immune neonatal unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia and rapid hemoglobin drop requiring exchange transfusion; negative Coombs, ABO/Rh, G6PD, hemoglobin electrophoresis, Heinz body, and enzyme screens; heterozygous autosomal dominant mutation (classic for ANK1, where c.82C-to-T in exon 2 is a known pathogenic nonsense variant) with frequent de novo occurrence explaining the unaffected sister.

For: Inherited red cell membrane disorder that can cause non-immune neonatal hemolysis and jaundice.

For: Autosomal dominant (PIEZO1/KCNN4) non-immune hemolytic anemia with negative routine hemolysis screens.

For: Severe unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia in a newborn with negative hemolytic antibody and enzyme screens.

Against: Does not cause hemolytic anemia (cannot explain the acute drop in hemoglobin from 142 g/L to 112 g/L) and is autosomal recessive rather than caused by a single heterozygous mutation.

Alloimmune hemolysis, G6PD deficiency, other enzymopathies, and hemoglobinopathies are excluded directly by the negative laboratory panel.

Crigler-Najjar syndrome is excluded by the acute drop in hemoglobin (proving hemolysis) and the heterozygous mode of inheritance.

Hereditary elliptocytosis and xerocytosis are less consistent with severe exchange-requiring neonatal hemolysis from a single heterozygous exon 2 c.82C-to-T mutation.

Committing to hereditary spherocytosis, which fully explains severe non-immune neonatal hemolytic jaundice with a heterozygous membrane protein mutation.

FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Hereditary spherocytosis

13-year-old boy born to consanguineous parents with normal early development and a 3-year history of progressive ataxia, recurrent falls, and foot dragging: strongly indicates an autosomal recessive hereditary leukodystrophy or spastic ataxia.

Vanishing white matter disease (childhood ataxia with central nervous system hypomyelination / CACH)
