CASE: M033

KEY FINDINGS:

Severe acute neuropathic pain (stabbing, shock-like, burning) in the proximal and intermediate right arm developing approximately 21 days after dengue virus infection, confirmed by positive dengue IgM serology — classic post-infectious immune-mediated latency.

Dengue-associated Parsonage-Turner syndrome (neuralgic amyotrophy / post-infectious brachial plexitis)

Cervical radiculopathy (C5–C6)

Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS)

Focal/pharyngeal-cervical-brachial variant of Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS)

Neoplastic or compressive brachial plexopathy

EVIDENCE FOR AND AGAINST:

Dengue-associated Parsonage-Turner syndrome (neuralgic amyotrophy):

For: Classic biphasic pattern of severe acute proximal shoulder/arm neuropathic pain followed by patchy weakness and rapid amyotrophy in upper/middle trunk muscles (deltoid, infraspinatus, biceps); clear post-infectious trigger (~21 days post-dengue with positive IgM); acute denervation on ENMG; MRI signal abnormality localized to the upper and middle primary trunks; normal workup for other etiologies.

Against: Livedo and marked allodynia/thermal hyperesthesia are less typical than pure motor-predominant presentations, though autonomic and sensory fiber involvement can occur.

Cervical radiculopathy (C5–C6):

For: Pain, weakness in deltoid/infraspinatus/biceps, sensory loss, and reduced/absent reflexes in a C5–C6 distribution.

Against: MRI demonstrates signal changes in the brachial plexus trunks rather than cervical root compression; severe burning/shock-like pain with rapid amyotrophy and livedo 3 weeks post-dengue strongly favors immune-mediated plexitis over structural radiculopathy.

Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS):

For: Severe burning pain, allodynia, thermal hyperesthesia, and skin discoloration (livedo) with functional impairment.

Against: CRPS does not explain acute axonal denervation on ENMG, MRI signal changes in the upper and middle brachial plexus trunks, or selective proximal myotomal paresis and amyotrophy; there is also no preceding limb trauma.

Guillain-Barré syndrome (focal/brachial variant):

For: Post-infectious onset (~3 weeks after dengue virus infection), acute weakness, hypotonia, and areflexia.

Against: Strictly unilateral focal involvement of one brachial plexus with severe localized neuropathic pain, rapid amyotrophy, normal cranial nerves, and MRI signal changes confined to the right upper/middle trunks argue strongly against GBS, which is typically symmetric and polyradiculoneuropathic.

Neoplastic or compressive brachial plexopathy:

For: Unilateral brachial plexus pain, weakness, sensory deficits, and denervation on ENMG.

Against: No mass lesion on brachial plexus MRI, acute post-dengue onset, and preferential involvement of the upper/middle trunks (neoplastic plexopathy more often affects the lower trunk, as in Pancoast tumors).

EXCLUSIONS AND COMMITMENT:

Cervical radiculopathy and compressive/neoplastic plexopathy are excluded by the absence of a compressive or mass lesion and the direct localization of signal changes to the upper and middle primary trunks on MRI. CRPS is excluded because it cannot account for objective acute denervation on ENMG and brachial plexus MRI abnormalities. GBS is excluded by the strictly unilateral, painful, trunk-localized plexopathy with normal cranial nerves. By direct temporal association with confirmed dengue infection, classic severe neuropathic pain followed by proximal amyotrophy, ENMG evidence of acute denervation, and MRI confirmation of upper and middle trunk involvement, the diagnosis commits to dengue-associated Parsonage-Turner syndrome (neuralgic amyotrophy).

FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Dengue-associated Parsonage-Turner syndrome (post-infectious neuralgic amyotrophy / brachial plexitis)

Visceral leishmaniasis

Pilocytic astrocytoma (HRAS-mutant / Schimmelpenning syndrome–associated)

Ganglioglioma

Glioblastoma (IDH-wildtype, high-grade glioma)

Pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma (PXA)

Cystic meningioma

Pilocytic astrocytoma:

Moyamoya disease

Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD)

Primary CNS vasculitis or cardioembolic stroke

Congenital hypoplasia of the left internal carotid artery:

For: Young male with left MCA territory ischemic stroke and hypoplastic left ICA situated within a congenitally narrowed bony carotid canal (3 mm vs. 7 mm on the right); because the bony carotid canal forms around the embryonic ICA, canal hypoplasia definitively establishes congenital ICA hypoplasia; normal coagulation and cardiopulmonary exams.

For: Leading cause of ischemic stroke in adolescents and young adults; causes luminal narrowing of the ICA and distal MCA occlusion/embolism.

For: Causes ischemic stroke in adolescents/young adults with steno-occlusion of the distal ICA and proximal MCA (M1/M2).

Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD):

For: Non-atherosclerotic arteriopathy capable of causing ICA narrowing and ischemic stroke in younger patients.

Against: Acquired arteriopathy with a normal bony carotid canal, classic "string-of-beads" appearance, and strong female predominance.

For: Can cause MCA territory ischemic stroke in a 17-year-old.

Against: Cardiopulmonary exam and labs are normal, and neither explains left ICA hypoplasia inside a narrowed 3 mm bony carotid canal.
