CASE: M009
REASONING:

A 13-year-old boy presents with a painless, clearly demarcated, spherical, bony-hard swelling on the buccal aspect of the right maxillary alveolar ridge, with intact overlying mucosa and vital adjacent teeth.

The absence of a family history of Gardner syndrome helps lower suspicion for syndromic multiple osteomas.

CBCT demonstrates an exophytic, mushroom-like radiodense lesion continuous with both the cortical and trabecular bone, which is characteristic of a bony exostosis.

Histopathology confirms bony trabeculae separated by connective tissue without a cartilaginous cap (ruling out osteochondroma), explicitly consistent with a benign exostosis/hyperostosis.

Given the anatomic site (buccal aspect of the maxillary alveolar ridge) and benign clinicopathologic features, the most likely diagnosis is a maxillary buccal exostosis.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Maxillary buccal exostosis

CASE: M010
REASONING:

A 34-year-old woman presents with severe abnormal uterine bleeding, menorrhagia with clots, and signs of hemodynamic instability 2 months after a D&C for a missed abortion. Uterine instrumentation (D&C) is a major risk factor for acquired uterine vascular malformations.

Initial TVUS and MRI show an echogenic/mixed endometrial mass with increased vascularity and serpiginous vessel signals, narrowing the differential to retained products of conception (RPOC) versus a uterine arteriovenous malformation (AVM).

CTA demonstrates a cluster of vessels with early venous filling, which is the hallmark diagnostic feature of an arteriovenous shunt/malformation rather than simple RPOC.

Selective angiography confirms the diagnosis by identifying the left uterine artery as the feeding vessel supplying the vascular malformation.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Acquired uterine arteriovenous malformation (AVM)

CASE: M011
REASONING:

A 33-year-old male with risk factors for HIV (newly diagnosed during admission) presents with fever, weight loss, anorexia, and 5 days of abdominal pain localizing to the right lower quadrant, initially mimicking acute appendicitis.

Chest imaging reveals right upper lobe consolidation and a tree-in-bud pattern of bronchiolitis on CT, classic radiographic signs of active pulmonary tuberculosis.

Intraoperatively, he is found to have a perforated cecal mass with diffuse white lesions along the small intestine and peritonitis. The ileocecal region is the most common site of gastrointestinal tuberculosis.

Histopathology shows necrotizing granulomatous colitis and granulomatous lymphadenitis, and PCR is positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, confirming the diagnosis of intestinal (and disseminated) tuberculosis in an immunocompromised (HIV-positive) host.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Intestinal tuberculosis with cecal perforation (in the setting of disseminated tuberculosis and newly diagnosed HIV)

CASE: M012
REASONING:

An 84-year-old hospitalized male with right lower lobe pneumonia develops sudden, severe diffuse abdominal pain, hypotension, tachycardia, and a rapid drop in hemoglobin (13.1 to 8.8 g/dL in 24 hours), indicating acute intra-abdominal hemorrhage.

Abdominal CTA shows active bleeding and hemoperitoneum, and the patient undergoes splenectomy, pointing directly to spontaneous (atraumatic) splenic rupture—likely precipitated by increased intra-abdominal pressure (e.g., coughing from pneumonia) acting on an underlying splenic lesion.

Postsplenectomy histopathology reveals a 1 cm subcapsular nodule with no malignancy or infectious organisms. Among benign, non-infectious, subcapsular splenic nodules capable of causing spontaneous rupture, splenic hemangioma is the most common and classic etiology.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Spontaneous splenic rupture secondary to a splenic hemangioma

CASE: M013
REASONING:

A 22-year-old male presents with upper anterior spacing and forwardly placed upper front teeth, with a history of childhood thumb-sucking—a classic etiological factor for maxillary incisor proclination, anterior spacing, and increased overjet.

Intraoral exam and cephalometric radiographs confirm a Class I skeletal relationship and a Class I posterior buccal (molar) occlusion, establishing an Angle's Class I malocclusion.

Extraoral findings (convex facial profile, acute nasolabial angle, deep mentolabial sulcus, lip incompetency with a lower lip trap) combined with increased overjet, upper anterior spacing, and lower anterior crowding are characteristic of Angle's Class I malocclusion with proclined maxillary incisors (Dewey's Type 2 modification, with concurrent lower anterior crowding).
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Angle's Class I malocclusion with maxillary anterior proclination and spacing (Dewey's Type 2)

CASE: M014
REASONING:

A 24-year-old female presents with chronic lower urinary tract symptoms (dysuria, pollakiuria) since puberty and progressively worsening lower back pain without radiculopathy.

Normal urinalysis, normal renal function, and a normal renovesical ultrasound rule out urinary tract infection and primary urological structural pathology, pointing toward a neurogenic bladder etiology.

Lumbar MRI directly identifies the underlying cause: a low-lying conus medullaris at the S1–S2 level (normally terminating at L1–L2) tethered to a sacral spinal canal lipoma, accompanied by sacral hypoplasia and vertebral body scalloping.

The combination of progressive lumbago, neurogenic urinary dysfunction, and a low-lying conus tethered to a sacral lipoma in adulthood confirms adult tethered cord syndrome.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Tethered cord syndrome secondary to a sacral spinal lipoma

CASE: M015
REASONING:

A 41-year-old male presents with a 2-year history of insidiously progressive gait imbalance and a 1-year history of abnormal speech and occipital headache, with no history of alcohol use, toxin exposure, or offending medications.

Neurological examination demonstrates a pure cerebellar syndrome: broad-based ataxic gait, scanning speech, jerky horizontal nystagmus, dysmetria, dyssynergia, dysdiadochokinesia, positive rebound phenomenon, hypotonia, and pendular reflexes, with completely preserved motor strength, cognition, and sensation (arguing against spinocerebellar tracts/posterior column involvement such as Friedreich ataxia).

Brain CT confirms marked cerebellar atrophy.

Family history reveals two affected siblings with unaffected parents and unaffected offspring of a sibling, indicating a horizontal inheritance pattern consistent with autosomal recessive transmission.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Autosomal recessive hereditary cerebellar ataxia

CASE: M016
REASONING:

A 66-year-old woman presents with subacute, rapidly progressive cognitive decline, weakness, falls, aphasia, hallucinations, and SIADH, proving fatal within four months despite unremarkable infectious, autoimmune, metabolic, and paraneoplastic workups.

CSF analysis shows pleocytosis and elevated IL-10, a highly specific biomarker for primary CNS B-cell lymphoma.

Initial MRI reveals diffuse, non-enhancing T2/FLAIR hyperintensities without mass effect or restricted diffusion, which is the classic radiological signature of lymphomatosis cerebri—a rare, diffusely infiltrating variant of primary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL).

MR spectroscopy confirms a hypercellular neoplastic process (elevated choline:creatine ratio, decreased NAA), and follow-up MRI shows new diffusion restriction and hyperperfusion in the caudate and hippocampus (consistent with superimposed focal lymphoma progression or ischemia).

Autopsy confirmation of CD20-positive B cells establishes the diagnosis of lymphomatosis cerebri (primary CNS diffuse large B-cell lymphoma).
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Lymphomatosis cerebri (diffuse variant of primary central nervous system diffuse large B-cell lymphoma)
