CASE: M073
REASONING: The patient is a 48-year-old male with a 25-year history of chronic alcoholism (specifically high intake of red wine) and severe malnutrition presenting with acute-to-subacute neuropsychiatric decline (confusion, stupor, apathy), dysarthria, frontal release signs (bilateral grasp reflex, gestural perseveration), spastic hypertonia, astasia-abasia, and features of interhemispheric disconnection (inability to manipulate objects). Brain CT shows hypodensity of the splenium of the corpus callosum, and brain MRI demonstrates symmetrical T2/FLAIR and DWI hyperintensity with restricted diffusion (low ADC) throughout the corpus callosum, along with bilateral frontal cortical involvement. This clinical and neuroimaging triad—chronic alcoholism/malnutrition, acute encephalopathy with disconnection/frontal signs, and selective demyelination/necrosis of the corpus callosum—is classic for Marchiafava-Bignami disease.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Marchiafava-Bignami disease

CASE: M074
REASONING: The patient is a 39-year-old female who originally presented at age 24 with a left renal mass initially classified as papillary renal cell carcinoma without immunohistochemical confirmation, and then developed late retroperitoneal soft tissue recurrences with mild enhancement and cystic changes 15 years later. In young female patients, tumors with papillary architecture that are misdiagnosed as papillary renal cell carcinoma on routine histology without immunohistochemistry—and are characteristically known for very late recurrences or metastases (often 10–20+ years after nephrectomy)—are classic for Xp11.2 translocation (TFE3-rearranged) renal cell carcinoma, which requires immunohistochemistry (such as TFE3) for definitive confirmation.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Xp11.2 translocation renal cell carcinoma

CASE: M075
REASONING: The patient is a woman in her 30s with myasthenia gravis (on steroids and rituximab) presenting with acute bouts of dry cough for one week that prevented completion of a six-minute walk test. Extensive cardiopulmonary evaluation—including chest X-ray, high-resolution chest CT, transthoracic echocardiography, complete blood count/eosinophils, and baseline spirometry—is completely normal, effectively ruling out rituximab-induced interstitial lung disease, pneumonia, heart failure, and overt fixed airway obstruction. However, she has a strong atopic background (lifelong allergic diathesis with episodic sneezing and rhinorrhea), occupational exposure to wood dust (woodcarving factory), obesity, normal baseline spirometry, and cough triggered by exertion (during the six-minute walk test). This clinical picture is most consistent with cough-variant asthma.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Cough-variant asthma

CASE: M076
REASONING: The patient is a woman in her 50s who received one month of parenteral nutrition for intestinal obstruction and subsequently developed the classic triad of ophthalmoplegia (limited ocular motility in multiple directions, diplopia), ataxia (unsteady gait, positive Romberg), and mental status changes (dull comprehension and reaction), along with pinpoint pupils and refractory hiccups. Laboratory testing confirms low vitamin B1 (thiamine) levels, and repeat brain MRI shows characteristic symmetric hyperintense lesions in the bilateral thalami and periaqueductal gray matter of the midbrain that improved on follow-up imaging. These findings are diagnostic of non-alcoholic Wernicke encephalopathy secondary to thiamine-deficient parenteral nutrition.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Wernicke encephalopathy

CASE: M077
REASONING: The patient is a 25-year-old woman with a four-month history of severe nausea, vomiting, dysphagia, and 60-pound weight loss who developed acute binocular diplopia, horizontal nystagmus, esotropia, severe vision and hearing loss, areflexia, and a wide-based ataxic gait. Laboratory evaluation demonstrates profound thiamine deficiency (<7 pg/mL) alongside other vitamin deficiencies, and brain MRI shows symmetric increased T2 signal in the bilateral medial thalami and periaqueductal regions. This constellation of severe malnutrition/vomiting, ophthalmoplegia, nystagmus, ataxia, low thiamine, and classic thalamic/periaqueductal MRI signal abnormalities confirms non-alcoholic Wernicke encephalopathy.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Wernicke encephalopathy

CASE: M078
REASONING: The patient is an 8-year-old boy with a family history of multiple hereditary exostoses (MHE) and multiple sessile osteochondromas in the pelvis and lower limbs who presents with progressive leg pain and gait difficulty. Cross-sectional imaging reveals classic signs of malignant transformation of an osteochondroma in the fibula: irregular borders, heterogeneous bone matrix, a thickened enhancing cartilaginous cap (6 mm), heterogeneous T2 signal, and soft tissue infiltration. Bone biopsy confirms a malignant mesenchymal tumor proliferation with moderate cytonuclear atypia. In the setting of multiple hereditary exostoses, malignant degeneration of an osteochondroma is most commonly secondary chondrosarcoma.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Secondary chondrosarcoma

CASE: M079
REASONING: The patient is a 23-year-old male with a 10-year history of recurrent right upper quadrant pain, normal liver enzymes and bilirubin, and imaging showing multifocal saccular and fusiform dilatations of the intrahepatic bile ducts containing multiple intrahepatic stones (hepatolithiasis). Most importantly, MRCP demonstrates the pathognomonic "central dot sign," which represents portal vein radicles and hepatic artery branches surrounded by dilated intrahepatic bile ducts. This imaging hallmark is diagnostic of Caroli disease.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Caroli disease

CASE: M080
REASONING: The patient is a 13-year-old boy presenting with right iliac fossa pain and gastrointestinal symptoms (with a normal appendix on ultrasound), bradycardia, and Mobitz type II second-degree AV block on ECG. Laboratory studies reveal strong evidence of a recent Group A Streptococcal infection (markedly elevated antistreptolysin O titer of 1,450 IU/mL) along with markedly elevated inflammatory markers (ESR 70 mm/h, CRP 20.2 mg/dL). Echocardiography confirms carditis/valvulitis with mild-to-moderate aortic and mitral regurgitation and mildly decreased left ventricular function. Meeting the Jones criteria with evidence of antecedent streptococcal infection, echocardiographic carditis, conduction abnormality, and elevated acute-phase reactants establishes the diagnosis of acute rheumatic fever.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Acute rheumatic fever
