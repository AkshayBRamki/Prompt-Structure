CASE: M105
REASONING:

The patient is a 49-year-old male with adult-onset progressive focal right lower limb dystonia (responsive to sensory tricks), subtle cerebellar ataxia, horizontal nystagmus, and cerebellar atrophy on MRI, without visual loss, seizures, or cognitive decline.

Parental consanguinity (first cousins) and affected siblings with neurological/psychiatric illness and early death point strongly toward an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder.

Metabolic, Wilson disease (normal copper/ceruloplasmin), autoimmune, and paraneoplastic causes were ruled out.

Electron microscopy of the skin biopsy revealed granular osmiophilic inclusions (granular osmiophilic deposits, GRODs) and mitochondrial vacuolar changes, which are pathognomonic ultrastructural hallmarks of neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis (NCL).

Adult-onset NCL without visual impairment presenting with progressive motor symptoms (dystonia, ataxia) and cerebellar atrophy corresponds clinically to Kufs disease (specifically adult-onset neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis).
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Adult-onset neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis (Kufs disease)

CASE: M106
REASONING:

The patient is a 38-year-old male with a 7-month history of a firm, slightly tender left inguinal mass and progressive ipsilateral leg swelling that failed to resolve despite 6 months of anticoagulation for presumed proximal DVT.

Doppler ultrasound and CT venography showed a cystic-appearing lesion compressing the left common femoral vein (CFV) with sluggish venous flow and no intraluminal DVT in the main lumen.

Intraoperative exploration demonstrated no extramural mass (ruling out ganglion/synovial cyst, lymphadenopathy, or hernia) and instead revealed an intrinsic vascular lesion with thrombosis.

A thrombosed venous aneurysm of the common femoral vein classically mimics an inguinal mass or extramural cystic lesion on imaging and causes venous outflow obstruction and persistent limb swelling.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Thrombosed left common femoral vein aneurysm

CASE: M107
REASONING:

The patient is a 3-year-old boy with central and peripheral cyanosis, failure to thrive, recurrent respiratory infections, single S2, systolic/diastolic murmurs, and hepatomegaly.

Chest radiography shows cardiomegaly, an absent main pulmonary segment, and increased pulmonary vascularity, pointing toward a conotruncal cyanotic congenital heart defect with increased pulmonary blood flow.

Echocardiography, multislice CT angiography, and cardiac catheterization confirm a single arterial trunk overriding a large VSD with a shared aorta and main pulmonary trunk (Truncus arteriosus Type I), truncal valve regurgitation, and pulmonary hypertension.

Imaging and catheterization also confirm an anomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA), further corroborated by lateral Q-waves on ECG.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Type I truncus arteriosus with anomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA)

CASE: M108
REASONING:

The patient is a 40-year-old male presenting with dry gangrene of the right ring and little fingers and pre-gangrenous changes in the index and middle fingers, despite normal peripheral pulses and a normal arterial Doppler study, which argues against large-vessel occlusive disease.

Autoimmune causes (ANA, antiphospholipid antibodies) were negative.

Complete blood count revealed severe normocytic normochromic anemia (Hb 6 g/dL), marked leukocytosis (153,000/mm³), and marked thrombocytosis (969,000/mm³), with mild hepatosplenomegaly on ultrasound.

Peripheral blood smear, bone marrow examination, and a positive RT-PCR for BCR-ABL (Philadelphia chromosome) definitively confirm chronic-phase chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), with digital gangrene occurring as a rare initial manifestation due to microvascular occlusion from hyperleukocytosis/leukostasis and thrombocytosis.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Chronic myeloid leukemia (chronic phase) presenting with digital gangrene

CASE: M109
REASONING:

The patient is a 66-year-old female who initially presented with unilateral follicular conjunctivitis that temporarily improved with topical tobramycin-dexamethasone, then progressed 3 weeks later to a conjunctival phlyctenule and subsequently nodular scleritis (confirmed by anterior segment OCT and UBM).

An extensive systemic autoimmune and infectious workup was entirely negative except for positive HSV-2 IgG serology.

Negative conjunctival swabs for HSV-1, HSV-2, and adenovirus do not exclude herpetic ocular disease, as herpetic scleritis and phlyctenular reactions are predominantly immune-mediated stromal/scleral processes rather than active superficial epithelial shedding, and can be exacerbated or unmasked following topical corticosteroid use.

Given the unilateral presentation, progression from follicular conjunctivitis to phlyctenule and nodular scleritis, and isolated HSV-2 seropositivity, herpes simplex virus type 2 is the most likely underlying etiology.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2)-associated nodular scleritis (preceded by conjunctival phlyctenule)

CASE: M110
REASONING:

The patient is a 53-year-old male with three episodes of sudden loss of consciousness without prodromal or postictal features and immediate return to baseline, which is classic for cardiogenic syncope.

Neurologic (normal head CT, no epileptiform activity on EEG), structural cardiac (normal echocardiogram), metabolic/endocrine (normal electrolytes and TSH), and tachyarrhythmic (non-inducible EPS) causes were ruled out.

EKG showed sinus bradycardia and sinus pauses, and inpatient telemetry captured a prolonged 8-second sinus pause (heart rate 37 bpm), directly explaining the syncopal episodes as sinus node dysfunction (sick sinus syndrome / sinus arrest).

In the absence of structural heart disease or intrinsic conduction defects, the patient's daily chronic marijuana use (combined with chronic sleep deprivation from night-shift work) is a well-recognized trigger for vagally mediated sinus arrest and sick sinus syndrome.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Cannabis-induced sick sinus syndrome (sinus arrest) causing cardiogenic syncope

CASE: M111
REASONING:

The patient is a 57-year-old male with p-ANCA-associated glomerulonephritis on chronic steroids who developed acute febrile, disseminated hemorrhagic and necrotic cutaneous lesions, mucosal erosions, pulmonary consolidation with respiratory failure, neutrophilic leukocytosis, and elevated CRP.

Skin biopsy showed a dense dermal neutrophilic infiltrate containing pseudocapsulated yeast-like structures closely mimicking Cryptococcus.

Crucially, fungal and bacterial stains and cultures were negative, while the yeast-like bodies stained strongly positive for myeloperoxidase (MPO), proving they are degenerating/vacuolated neutrophils rather than true fungal organisms.

This histopathologic triad (dermal neutrophilic infiltrate, MPO-positive pseudocapsulated Cryptococcus-like bodies, and negative fungal workup) in a febrile patient with hemorrhagic skin lesions and pulmonary involvement is pathognomonic for cryptococcoid Sweet syndrome (an uncommon variant of acute febrile neutrophilic dermatosis).
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Cryptococcoid Sweet syndrome

CASE: M112
REASONING:

The patient is a 65-year-old male with diabetes and prior stroke presenting with a 7-day severe right-sided headache, recurrent transient episodes of left-sided numbness and weakness (crescendo TIAs), and progression to persistent left hemiparesis, left facial/lingual paresis, sensory loss, dysarthria, and a left Babinski sign.

Cranio-cervical CTA demonstrated critical (95%) stenosis at the origin of the right internal carotid artery, directly corresponding to the right hemispheric symptoms.

Brain MRI with DWI confirmed acute ischemic infarcts (multiple scattered hyperintensities in the right hemisphere), and SWI showed asymmetrically prominent cortical veins (APCV) on the right side, a classic imaging marker of severe cerebral hypoperfusion (misery perfusion) with increased deoxyhemoglobin extraction.

Together, these findings establish an acute right hemispheric ischemic stroke driven by hemodynamic hypoperfusion and/or artery-to-artery embolism from severe right internal carotid artery stenosis.
FINAL DIAGNOSIS: Acute right hemispheric ischemic stroke secondary to severe right internal carotid artery stenosis
